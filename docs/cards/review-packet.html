What we are asking you to do

These are multiple-choice cards for an AI-assisted ground school for students aged roughly 16 to 26, most of them new to aviation. No student has seen any of them. Nothing reaches a student until a CFI approves it by name, and that is what this packet is for.

For each card, please judge three things:

  1. Is it accurate? If a card is wrong, or right but misleading, say so. "Cut this one" is a useful answer.
  2. Are the wrong answers realistic? Each wrong option is meant to be a mistake a real student pilot makes. An obviously silly option teaches nothing — the student picks the right answer by elimination and learns that they are good at tests.
  3. Does the card test the objective it sits under? The objective is printed above each group of cards.

Two things we need you to fill in, marked in the text:

  • value needed — a number that is aircraft-specific, or one we were not certain of. We left a gap rather than guess. Please fill it in, or tell us the card reads better with no number at all. 4 cards carry one.
  • Our own doubt about this card — a specific question we have written down for you. 4 cards carry one.

The options are shuffled every time a student sees a card. The A–D letters here exist only so we can talk about them; no student sees this order. Each option also has a permanent id (opt-1 to opt-4) that does not change when the order does, so please use the id in any note about a specific option.

Two consequences of the shuffling, worth knowing if you rewrite an option: an option must never refer to another by letter, and there is no "all of the above" or "none of the above" — they depend on there being an above.

Sources are named, never numbered: no section numbers, ACS task codes, or figure numbers anywhere. A student who repeats a stale code to a DPE pays for our mistake.

Card 1

What IMSAFE is checking

s1-imsafe-pave.imsafe-checklist.c1

IMSAFE is a checklist you run on what?

  1. A The airplane's required equipment and paperwork opt-1
  2. B Yourself — whether you are fit to fly today opt-2 correct answer
  3. C The weather, the route, and the destination opt-3
  4. D The maintenance items due before the next flight opt-4

Shown after answering: Everything else you check before a flight is about the airplane or the conditions. IMSAFE is the one that looks at the pilot: Illness, Medication, Stress, Alcohol, Fatigue, Emotion. You may hear an instructor give the E as Eating — that version comes from other aviation authorities and some training groups rather than the FAA, so it is worth knowing it exists, but Emotion is the one we teach. It is the first real pilot decision of the day, and the only one nobody else can make for you. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

On the right, the same pilot standing still with a hand on their own chest, labelled "IMSAFE." Six small tiles beneath the right-hand figure, one per letter, with the word spelled out. No airplane on the right-hand side at all — the absence is the point.

Card 4

The four buckets

s1-imsafe-pave.pave-checklist.c1

PAVE sorts the risks of a flight into four areas. Which four?

  1. A Preflight, Airspace, Visibility, Emergency planning opt-1
  2. B Planning, Aircraft, Weather, Experience opt-2
  3. C Pilot, Aircraft, Environment, External pressures opt-3 correct answer
  4. D Pilot, Altitude, Visibility, Equipment opt-4

Shown after answering: PAVE gives you four places to look so that a risk does not go unnoticed simply because you were thinking about something else. Pilot is you, Aircraft is the machine, Environment is the weather, terrain, airport and time of day, and External pressures are the reasons you feel you have to go. Card 5

Friends waiting at the destination

s1-imsafe-pave.pave-checklist.c2

You told friends you would be on the ground by a certain time, and they are waiting for you there. Where does that belong in PAVE?

  1. A Pilot opt-1
  2. B External pressures opt-2 correct answer
  3. C Environment opt-3
  4. D Aircraft opt-4

Shown after answering: A promise you have made to somebody else is a reason to keep going when you should turn back, and PAVE gives that its own letter precisely because it is so easy to miss — it is the only one of the four that is not about the flight at all. Recognising it is what stops it deciding for you. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

Picture we plan to draw: A pilot in the cockpit at the top of the frame with a thought bubble containing a clock and two waiting figures. Our own doubt about this card: The Pilot and External-pressures boundary is genuinely arguable here, and a thoughtful student could defend the Pilot option: it is your feeling of obligation, and that feeling lives in you. We think the point of the E is that the pressure originates outside you, and that filing it under Pilot is how it gets lost. Please tell us whether that distinction is the one you teach, and whether this card sharpens it or muddies it.

  1. A Environment opt-1 correct answer

Naming the attitude

s1-imsafe-pave.hazardous-attitudes.c1

  2. B Invulnerability opt-2

Shown after answering: Anti-authority is the attitude that the rules are somebody else's problem. It is worth separating from Macho, which is about proving something, and from Invulnerability, which is about believing the bad outcome happens to other people. Card 10

What actually causes a stall

s1-stalls.stall-any-airspeed.c1

What makes a wing stall?

  1. A The wing passes its critical angle of attack opt-2 correct answer
  2. B The airplane slows below a fixed speed printed in the handbook opt-1
  3. C The engine loses power opt-3
  4. D The nose is pointed too far above the horizon opt-4

Shown after answering: A stall is about the angle between the wing and the air coming at it — the angle of attack — not about speed. Past a certain angle, called the critical angle of attack, the air stops following the top of the wing and lift falls off. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge and the Airplane Flying Handbook.

Picture we plan to draw: Side view of a wing with the relative wind drawn as arrows coming from ahead. A shaded angle between the wing's chord line and that relative wind, labelled "angle of attack." Three frames: small angle with smooth attached airflow; larger angle, still attached; past the critical angle, with the airflow over the top breaking away into turbulent swirls. The airspeed reading stays identical in all three frames, to make the point that speed did not change anything.

Stalling fast

What it feels like first

Card 16

The first move

s1-stalls.stall-recovery-spins.c1

What is the first thing you do to recover from a stall?

  1. A Add full power opt-1
  2. B Level the wings with aileron opt-3
  3. C Pull back to stop the airplane descending opt-4
  4. D Reduce the angle of attack — release the back pressure and lower the nose opt-2 correct answer

Shown after answering: Only reducing the angle of attack makes the wing fly again — everything else comes after. The pull-back-to-stop-the-descent option is the instinct that gets people hurt: pulling harder is exactly what deepens a stall. Power and wings-level follow, in the order your airplane's procedure gives. Confirm in the Airplane Flying Handbook, and follow the POH or AFM for your airplane.

Card 17

What turns a stall into a spin

s1-stalls.stall-recovery-spins.c2

  4. D An engine failure opt-4

Shown after answering: A spin needs two ingredients at once: a stalled wing, and yaw.
