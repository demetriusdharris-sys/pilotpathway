# Quiz cards for review — Stage 1, "Welcome to the flight deck"

**Lesson:** `s1-welcome` · **ACS area of operation:** Preflight Preparation · **Sources drawn on:** 14 CFR, PHAK

**Status: DRAFT. Not shown to any student until a CFI signs off.**

## What we are asking you to do

Nine cards, three for each of the lesson's three learning objectives. None is marked safety-critical, but this is the first lesson a student meets, and the third objective is about the limits of the AI they are talking to — which we would rather a CFI checked than anyone here.

Please check three things:

1. **Is it accurate?** Especially the training path, which differs between a Part 141 school and Part 61 training, and where we have deliberately described the shape rather than the requirements.
2. **Are the wrong answers realistic?** Each is meant to be something someone new to aviation genuinely assumes.
3. **What should be cut?**

Markers in the text: **`[CFI: confirm value]`** is a number we would not guess. **`FLAG FOR CFI:`** is a specific doubt of ours, written down.

No section numbers, ACS task codes, or figure numbers appear anywhere. Sources are named only.

## About the A–D letters

**The options are shuffled every time a card is shown to a student.** The letters here are display only. Each option carries a stable id, `opt-1` to `opt-4`, and **Correct answer** names that id rather than a letter. Never write an option that refers to another by letter, and there is no "all of the above" or "none of the above".

The ids do not run in alphabetical order down a card. An option keeps its id when the options are reordered.

There is a blank **CFI review** line at the end of every card.

## Objective 1 — `s1-welcome.training-path`

> Describe the path from first lesson to private pilot certificate

### Card 1 — What the path is made of

**Objective ID:** `s1-welcome.training-path`

**Question:** Which of these describes the shape of private pilot training?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-2`) Lessons with an instructor, then supervised solo flying, then cross-country flying — with a knowledge test and a practical test to finish
- **B.** (`opt-4`) A written exam, and then a certificate is issued
- **C.** (`opt-1`) A fixed number of weeks in a classroom, then a single flight
- **D.** (`opt-3`) Solo flight from the first lesson, with an instructor only for the test

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** Ground knowledge and flying grow together: dual lessons, then solo flight once your instructor endorses it, then cross-country work, and two tests — a knowledge test on the ground and a practical test in the airplane. How long each part takes is personal, and comparing your hours to someone else's tells you very little. The exact requirements depend on whether you train under Part 61 or at a Part 141 school. Confirm in 14 CFR and with your CFI.

### Card 2 — Two different tests

**Objective ID:** `s1-welcome.training-path`

**Question:** The knowledge test and the practical test — what is the difference?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) They are the same test, taken twice
- **B.** (`opt-4`) The practical test is a longer written exam
- **C.** (`opt-2`) The knowledge test is optional if you pass the practical test
- **D.** (`opt-3`) The knowledge test is taken on the ground; the practical test includes an oral portion and a flight with an examiner

**Correct answer:** `opt-3`

**Explanation:** The knowledge test is a written exam you sit at a testing centre. The practical test — the checkride — has two parts: an oral discussion and a flight, both with an examiner. Passing the written is not the finish line; it is one of the things that has to be in place before the checkride. Confirm in 14 CFR and with your CFI.

**Visual:** Two panels: a testing-centre desk with a screen, and a cockpit with two seats and an examiner. A single certificate drawn beyond both, reached only by a line passing through each in turn.

### Card 3 — How long it takes

**Objective ID:** `s1-welcome.training-path`

**Question:** Another student got their certificate in far fewer hours than you are on track for. What does that tell you?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) That you are behind and should consider whether flying is for you
- **B.** (`opt-4`) Very little — training time varies with how often you fly, the weather, the aircraft, and what you are working on
- **C.** (`opt-2`) That their instructor skipped required training
- **D.** (`opt-3`) That the regulations were changed between the two of you

**Correct answer:** `opt-4`

**Explanation:** Flying every week builds skill faster than flying once a month; a winter of weather or an airplane in maintenance stretches a timeline; and the minimum hours in the regulations are a floor, not a target most people meet. The standard you are being measured against is fixed and public. The time it takes to reach it is not, and it is not a measure of whether you belong here. Confirm in 14 CFR and with your CFI.

**Visual:** Two paths to the same destination drawn side by side, one with evenly spaced steps and one with gaps and doubled-back sections, both arriving at the same point. No figure is drawn as ahead or behind.

## Objective 2 — `s1-welcome.what-is-the-acs`

> Explain what the ACS is and how it sets the standard

### Card 4 — What the ACS is

**Objective ID:** `s1-welcome.what-is-the-acs`

**Question:** What is the Airman Certification Standards document?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) A schedule of lesson prices set by flight schools
- **B.** (`opt-2`) A list of aircraft approved for training
- **C.** (`opt-3`) The published standard for what you must know, consider, and be able to do to pass the test
- **D.** (`opt-4`) A record of your progress kept by your instructor

**Correct answer:** `opt-3`

**Explanation:** The ACS sets out, in the open, what an applicant must know, the risks they must be able to manage, and the skills they must demonstrate. It is not a secret held by examiners — you can read the same document they use. Knowing that the standard is public, and the same for everyone, is worth something on the days training feels like guesswork. Confirm in the Airman Certification Standards and with your CFI.

**Visual:** A single open document with three labelled columns — knowledge, risk management, skill — and two readers on either side of it, a student and an examiner, both looking at the same page.

### Card 5 — Who the standard bends for

**Objective ID:** `s1-welcome.what-is-the-acs`

**Question:** Does the standard in the ACS change depending on the student?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-2`) Yes — examiners adjust it for a student's background and circumstances
- **B.** (`opt-4`) No — the standard is the same for every applicant, whatever their route to it
- **C.** (`opt-1`) Yes — it is lower for students training at smaller schools
- **D.** (`opt-3`) Yes — it depends on the aircraft you train in

**Correct answer:** `opt-4`

**Explanation:** The standard does not move. What can and should differ is the teaching: the examples used, the pace, the support, the cost of getting to the airport. **Nobody is served by a pilot certificate that meant something different for them than for the person next to them** — least of all the pilot holding it. Confirm in the Airman Certification Standards.

**Visual:** A single horizontal line labelled as the standard, with several different routes climbing to it from different starting heights. Every route reaches the same line; none of them stops short.

### Card 6 — Tolerances

**Objective ID:** `s1-welcome.what-is-the-acs`

**Question:** The ACS gives tolerances — how far from a target altitude or heading you may be and still meet the standard. What are they for?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-3`) They set out what "good enough" means, in numbers, so it is not a matter of opinion on the day
- **B.** (`opt-1`) They are suggestions, and examiners commonly ignore them
- **C.** (`opt-4`) They describe the maximum the aircraft is capable of
- **D.** (`opt-2`) They apply only to the knowledge test

**Correct answer:** `opt-3`

**Explanation:** Nobody flies a perfect altitude forever, so the standard says how much variation is acceptable and expects you to notice and correct. Having that written down protects you: it makes passing a matter of meeting a published standard rather than of impressing someone. The tolerances themselves are in the ACS: `[CFI: confirm value]`. Confirm in the Airman Certification Standards.

**Visual:** An altitude trace wandering slightly above and below a target line, with a shaded band around the line showing the tolerance and the trace staying inside it — including the corrections back toward the middle.

## Objective 3 — `s1-welcome.cfi-vs-ai`

> State what a human CFI must do that an AI instructor cannot

### Card 7 — Who signs

**Objective ID:** `s1-welcome.cfi-vs-ai`

**Question:** Captain Path has worked through every lesson with you and you can answer anything it asks. Who can endorse you for solo flight?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-2`) Captain Path, once you have completed the lessons
- **B.** (`opt-1`) The flight school's office, on the instructor's behalf
- **C.** (`opt-3`) You, once you are confident you are ready
- **D.** (`opt-4`) An authorized flight instructor, and nobody else

**Correct answer:** `opt-4`

**Explanation:** An endorsement is a certificated instructor putting their own certificate behind a judgment that you are ready — made by someone who has flown with you. No amount of ground knowledge substitutes for that, and this app cannot give it, will not claim to, and would be worth less if it pretended otherwise. What it can do is make sure the knowledge part is solid before you get in the airplane. Confirm in 14 CFR and with your CFI.

**Visual:** A logbook endorsement block with a pen and a signature, and beside it a screen showing a chat — the screen drawn with no pen and no signature line at all.

### Card 8 — What the AI is for

**Objective ID:** `s1-welcome.cfi-vs-ai`

**Question:** What is an AI ground instructor genuinely useful for?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-3`) Replacing flight lessons with an instructor
- **B.** (`opt-2`) Deciding whether you are ready for your checkride
- **C.** (`opt-1`) Working through knowledge at your own pace, at any hour, as many times as you need
- **D.** (`opt-4`) Signing off the flight time in your logbook

**Correct answer:** `opt-1`

**Explanation:** Ground knowledge is where an AI is genuinely good: it is available at midnight, it does not get tired of a question asked four different ways, and it costs nothing per hour — which matters when instruction is billed by the hour. Flight instruction, endorsements, and any judgment about your readiness belong to a human instructor. Confirm with your CFI.

**Visual:** Two columns of equal size, headed "on the ground" and "in the airplane", with a phone in the first and a cockpit with two seats in the second. Neither column is drawn larger than the other.

### Card 9 — When the app and the instructor disagree

**Objective ID:** `s1-welcome.cfi-vs-ai`

**Question:** Something Captain Path told you does not match what your CFI says. What do you do?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-2`) Go with your CFI, and raise the difference with them — they know your airplane, your airport, and you
- **B.** (`opt-4`) Go with the app, since it can search more material
- **C.** (`opt-1`) Pick whichever you find easier to remember
- **D.** (`opt-3`) Ignore both until an examiner settles it

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** Your instructor is accountable for your training and knows the specifics this app cannot — the aircraft, the field, the local procedures, and what you did last Tuesday. Bringing the disagreement to them is also how errors here get found and fixed. Asking "my ground school said this, why is it different?" is a good question, not a challenge. Confirm with your CFI and the current FAA handbooks.

**Visual:** Two speech bubbles meeting at a student figure, one from a screen and one from an instructor, with the student's next line drawn as a question mark directed at the instructor rather than at either bubble.
