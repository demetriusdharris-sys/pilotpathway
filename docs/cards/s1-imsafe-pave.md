# Quiz cards for review — Stage 1, "IMSAFE and PAVE — should we fly?"

**Lesson:** `s1-imsafe-pave` · **ACS area of operation:** Preflight Preparation · **Sources drawn on:** PHAK, AIM

**Status: DRAFT. Not shown to any student until a CFI signs off.**

## What we are asking you to do

Nine cards, three for each of the lesson's three learning objectives. All three objectives are marked safety-critical, which is why these are going to a human before they go anywhere near a student.

Please check three things:

1. **Is it accurate?** If a card is wrong, or right but misleading, mark it. Being told "cut this one" is a good outcome.
2. **Are the wrong answers realistic?** Each wrong option is meant to be a mistake a real student pilot actually makes. If one is obviously silly, it teaches nothing — a student picks the right answer by elimination and learns that they are good at tests.
3. **What should be cut?** Nine cards for one lesson may be too many. Say which earn their place.

Two things you will see in the text, deliberately:

- **`[CFI: confirm value]`** — a number that is aircraft-specific, situation-specific, or that we were not certain of. We left a gap rather than guess. Please fill it in, or tell us the card is better without a number at all.
- **`FLAG FOR CFI:`** — we have a specific doubt about that card and have written down what it is.

No section numbers, ACS task codes, or figure numbers appear anywhere. Sources are named only — a student who shows a DPE a stale code pays for our mistake.

## About the A–D letters

**The options are shuffled every time a card is shown to a student.** The letters here are display only — they exist so we can talk about the options in this document, and no student will ever see them in this order. Each option therefore also carries a stable id, `opt-1` to `opt-4`, and **Correct answer** names that id rather than a letter.

Two things follow from this, and they matter when you write or fix an option:

- **Never write an option that refers to another one by letter.** "Both A and B," "same as C but without the medication," "A or D" — all of these become nonsense once the order changes. If two options need to relate to each other, say it in words that survive being moved.
- **No "all of the above" or "none of the above."** Those depend on there being an above.

The ids do not run in alphabetical order down a card. An option keeps its own id when the options are reordered, so seeing **A.** (`opt-2`) is expected, not a typo.

If you need to reference an option in your review notes, use its id — `opt-3` means the same thing tomorrow, after a reshuffle, and in the database.

**One exception worth stating plainly:** IMSAFE and PAVE are checklists whose letters are fixed and are part of the content. A question asking what the S in IMSAFE stands for is a question about the checklist, not a positional reference to option S. Those are fine and will not break under shuffling.

There is a blank **CFI review** line at the end of every card.

## Objective 1 — `s1-imsafe-pave.imsafe-checklist`

> Apply the IMSAFE checklist to your own condition before a flight

### Card 1 — What IMSAFE is checking

**Objective ID:** `s1-imsafe-pave.imsafe-checklist`

**Question:** IMSAFE is a checklist you run on what?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) The airplane's required equipment and paperwork
- **B.** (`opt-2`) Yourself — whether you are fit to fly today
- **C.** (`opt-3`) The weather, the route, and the destination
- **D.** (`opt-4`) The maintenance items due before the next flight

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** Everything else you check before a flight is about the airplane or the conditions. IMSAFE is the one that looks at the pilot: **I**llness, **M**edication, **S**tress, **A**lcohol, **F**atigue, **E**motion. It is the first real pilot decision of the day, and the only one nobody else can make for you. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** A split graphic. On the left, a preflight walkaround silhouette with a clipboard, labelled "everything else you check." On the right, the same pilot standing still with a hand on their own chest, labelled "IMSAFE." Six small tiles beneath the right-hand figure, one per letter, with the word spelled out. No airplane on the right-hand side at all — the absence is the point.

**FLAG FOR CFI:** Please confirm the expansion we are teaching for the final letter. We have written **E — Emotion**, which is the version we believe the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge uses, but some instructors teach **Eating**, and a few teach both. Whatever you tell us goes into every later card and into the tutor's own answers, so it is worth getting settled once. If the answer is "both are taught, here is which one we use," say so and we will make the card reflect that.

### Card 2 — The letter students most often miss

**Objective ID:** `s1-imsafe-pave.imsafe-checklist`

**Question:** In IMSAFE, what does the S stand for?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) Sleep
- **B.** (`opt-2`) Situation
- **C.** (`opt-3`) Skill
- **D.** (`opt-4`) Stress

**Correct answer:** `opt-4`

**Explanation:** Stress — money, school, family, a hard week — rides along in the cockpit whether or not you invite it, and it eats the attention you need for flying. Sleep is a genuinely easy one to land on, but sleep belongs to Fatigue, which has its own letter. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** The six IMSAFE letters shown as a vertical column with each expansion written out, and the S row highlighted. Beside the S row, three small icons — a phone with unread messages, a bill, a clock showing a late hour — drawn small and ordinary rather than dramatic. Beside the F row, a separate icon of a bed, drawn to make the Sleep-versus-Stress distinction visible at a glance.

### Card 3 — A head cold on lesson day

**Objective ID:** `s1-imsafe-pave.imsafe-checklist`

**Question:** You wake up with a heavy head cold on the morning of a lesson. What does IMSAFE ask you to weigh?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) Illness is its own item, and a cold can affect your ears, your sinuses and your judgment
- **B.** (`opt-2`) Only whether you have a fever — without one, a cold is not an IMSAFE item
- **C.** (`opt-3`) Nothing, as long as you take something for it before you go
- **D.** (`opt-4`) Only conditions your aviation medical examiner already knows about

**Correct answer:** `opt-1`

**Explanation:** A cold is exactly what the I is for — blocked sinuses and ears hurt on the way down, and feeling rough dulls the judgment you are there to practise. Reaching for medication does not clear the checklist either; that is the M, and many over-the-counter remedies carry their own wait before flight — `[CFI: confirm value]`. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** A simple head-in-profile diagram showing the sinus cavities and the middle ear, with small pressure arrows on a descending flight path beside it. Underneath, two checklist rows side by side — Illness and Medication — with a line drawn between them and the caption "treating one lands you in the other."

## Objective 2 — `s1-imsafe-pave.pave-checklist`

> Apply the PAVE checklist to pilot, aircraft, environment, and external pressure

### Card 4 — The four buckets

**Objective ID:** `s1-imsafe-pave.pave-checklist`

**Question:** PAVE sorts the risks of a flight into four areas. Which four?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) Preflight, Airspace, Visibility, Emergency planning
- **B.** (`opt-2`) Planning, Aircraft, Weather, Experience
- **C.** (`opt-3`) Pilot, Aircraft, Environment, External pressures
- **D.** (`opt-4`) Pilot, Altitude, Visibility, Equipment

**Correct answer:** `opt-3`

**Explanation:** PAVE gives you four places to look so that a risk does not go unnoticed simply because you were thinking about something else. Pilot is you, Aircraft is the machine, Environment is the weather, terrain, airport and time of day, and External pressures are the reasons you feel you have to go. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** Four quadrants of equal size, each labelled with its word and carrying one plain icon: a pilot's head and shoulders; a small trainer aircraft; a cloud over a ridgeline with a moon; and a phone showing a waiting message. Equal weight given to all four quadrants, with no arrows or ranking between them.

### Card 5 — Friends waiting at the destination

**Objective ID:** `s1-imsafe-pave.pave-checklist`

**Question:** You told friends you would be on the ground by a certain time, and they are waiting for you there. Where does that belong in PAVE?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) Pilot
- **B.** (`opt-2`) External pressures
- **C.** (`opt-3`) Environment
- **D.** (`opt-4`) Aircraft

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** A promise you have made to somebody else is a reason to keep going when you should turn back, and PAVE gives that its own letter precisely because it is so easy to miss — it is the only one of the four that is not about the flight at all. Recognising it is what stops it deciding for you. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**FLAG FOR CFI:** The Pilot and External-pressures boundary is genuinely arguable here, and a thoughtful student could defend the Pilot option: it is your feeling of obligation, and that feeling lives in you. We think the point of the E is that the pressure originates outside you, and that filing it under Pilot is how it gets lost. Please tell us whether that distinction is the one you teach, and whether this card sharpens it or muddies it.

**Visual:** A pilot in the cockpit at the top of the frame with a thought bubble containing a clock and two waiting figures. The four PAVE quadrants sit below, with a dotted line running from the thought bubble past the Pilot quadrant and into External pressures, captioned "it feels like it is about you — it is not."

### Card 6 — Night, terrain, unfamiliar field

**Objective ID:** `s1-imsafe-pave.pave-checklist`

**Question:** The flight is at night, into an airport with rising ground nearby. Which PAVE area covers the darkness and the terrain?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) Environment
- **B.** (`opt-2`) Pilot
- **C.** (`opt-3`) External pressures
- **D.** (`opt-4`) Aircraft

**Correct answer:** `opt-1`

**Explanation:** Environment is everything the flight happens in rather than everything you bring to it — weather, terrain, the airport itself, and the time of day. Darkness and rising ground are both squarely in that bucket. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge and the Aeronautical Information Manual.

**FLAG FOR CFI:** We deliberately kept the wording to the darkness and the terrain, because "an unfamiliar airport" would have straddled two buckets — the field is Environment, but *your* lack of familiarity with it is Pilot. That overlap is real and arguably the more useful thing to teach. Please say whether to keep this card narrow as written, or to rewrite it to make the overlap the whole point.

**Visual:** A night approach drawn in profile: an airplane on a descending path, a ridgeline rising beneath and ahead of it, an airport beacon on the far side of the ridge. Along the bottom, the four PAVE labels with Environment highlighted. The pilot figure is drawn outside the scene, off to one side, to keep the two ideas visually separate.

## Objective 3 — `s1-imsafe-pave.hazardous-attitudes`

> Describe the hazardous attitudes and their antidotes

### Card 7 — Naming the attitude

**Objective ID:** `s1-imsafe-pave.hazardous-attitudes`

**Question:** "Those rules are for people who do not really know what they are doing." Which hazardous attitude is that?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) Impulsivity
- **B.** (`opt-2`) Invulnerability
- **C.** (`opt-3`) Macho
- **D.** (`opt-4`) Anti-authority

**Correct answer:** `opt-4`

**Explanation:** Anti-authority is the attitude that the rules are somebody else's problem. It is worth separating from Macho, which is about proving something, and from Invulnerability, which is about believing the bad outcome happens to other people. They can appear together in the same sentence, which is why naming them matters. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** A regulation page or a placarded limitation shown at an angle, with a hand pushing it aside. Beside it, the five attitude names listed plainly, with Anti-authority marked. No caricature of a pilot — the point is that this is a thought, not a personality.

### Card 8 — Matching the antidote

**Objective ID:** `s1-imsafe-pave.hazardous-attitudes`

**Question:** Which thought is the antidote to Invulnerability?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) "Taking chances is foolish."
- **B.** (`opt-2`) "Not so fast. Think first."
- **C.** (`opt-3`) "It could happen to me."
- **D.** (`opt-4`) "I am not helpless. I can make a difference."

**Correct answer:** `opt-3`

**Explanation:** Invulnerability is the belief that accidents happen to other people, so its antidote is the sentence that puts you back in the picture. Every option here is a real antidote to one of the five attitudes, which is the point — knowing the list is not the same as matching it. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** Five rows, each pairing an attitude with its antidote, laid out as a simple matching table with the Invulnerability row highlighted. The other four rows stay fully legible rather than greyed out, so the card teaches the whole set even while asking about one.

### Card 9 — The quiet one

**Objective ID:** `s1-imsafe-pave.hazardous-attitudes`

**Question:** "There is nothing I can do about it. Whatever happens, happens." Which hazardous attitude is that?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) Resignation
- **B.** (`opt-2`) Impulsivity
- **C.** (`opt-3`) Anti-authority
- **D.** (`opt-4`) Macho

**Correct answer:** `opt-1`

**Explanation:** Resignation is the one that does not look like a hazardous attitude, because it sounds like modesty rather than risk-taking. It shows up as a pilot who stops trying to influence the outcome — and a student who decides early that aviation is not really for people like them is closer to this one than to any of the others. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** A pair of hands coming off a control yoke, palms open. Beside it, an altimeter or a fuel gauge drawn mid-problem and unattended. Deliberately understated — no alarm colouring — because the card is about an attitude that does not announce itself.
