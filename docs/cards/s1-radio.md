# Quiz cards for review — Stage 1, "Talking on the radio without freezing"

**Lesson:** `s1-radio` · **ACS area of operation:** Airport and Seaplane Base Operations · **Sources drawn on:** AIM

**Status: DRAFT. Not shown to any student until a CFI signs off.**

## What we are asking you to do

Nine cards, three for each of the lesson's three learning objectives. None is marked safety-critical, though two of them — what to do when you are unsure of an instruction, and how a nontowered field works — have safety consequences a student meets on their first solo.

Please check three things:

1. **Is it accurate?** Phraseology varies a little by region and by instructor. Where a card teaches a convention rather than a rule, tell us if the convention you teach differs.
2. **Are the wrong answers realistic?** Each wrong option is meant to be what a nervous student actually does on the radio.
3. **What should be cut?**

Markers in the text: **`[CFI: confirm value]`** is a number we would not guess. **`FLAG FOR CFI:`** is a specific doubt of ours, written down.

No section numbers, ACS task codes, or figure numbers appear anywhere. Sources are named only.

## About the A–D letters

**The options are shuffled every time a card is shown to a student.** The letters here are display only. Each option carries a stable id, `opt-1` to `opt-4`, and **Correct answer** names that id rather than a letter. Never write an option that refers to another by letter, and there is no "all of the above" or "none of the above".

The ids do not run in alphabetical order down a card. An option keeps its id when the options are reordered.

There is a blank **CFI review** line at the end of every card.

## Objective 1 — `s1-radio.standard-call`

> Build a standard radio call: who you are calling, who you are, where you are, what you want

### Card 1 — The shape of a call

**Objective ID:** `s1-radio.standard-call`

**Question:** What is the order of a standard radio call?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-4`) What you want, who you are, where you are, who you are calling
- **B.** (`opt-1`) Where you are, what you want, who you are
- **C.** (`opt-3`) Who you are, what you want, and nothing else
- **D.** (`opt-2`) Who you are calling, who you are, where you are, what you want

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** The order is built so the listener can use each piece as it arrives: the facility name tells them the call is for them, your call sign tells them who is speaking, your position tells them where to look, and your request tells them what to do about it. Every call you will ever make — tower, ground, approach, a nontowered field — is that same shape. Confirm in the Aeronautical Information Manual.

**Visual:** Four stacked bars of a single call, each labelled with the question it answers: who am I calling, who am I, where am I, what do I want. Beside them, a controller figure with the same four labels arriving in order.

### Card 2 — Before you press the button

**Objective ID:** `s1-radio.standard-call`

**Question:** What should happen before you transmit?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) Listen first, and know what you are going to say
- **B.** (`opt-3`) Transmit immediately, so you are not beaten to it
- **C.** (`opt-4`) Write the whole call out word for word every time
- **D.** (`opt-2`) Wait until the frequency has been silent for a long while

**Correct answer:** `opt-1`

**Explanation:** Listening first tells you whether the frequency is busy, what the controller is working with, and often what they are about to ask you — and having the call planned in your head is what stops it coming out backwards. Transmitting over someone else means neither call is heard. Having the key words noted down is a normal thing to do while you are learning. Confirm in the Aeronautical Information Manual.

**Visual:** A cockpit view with the transmit button and a small thought bubble containing the four parts of the call already assembled. Beside it, a radio waveform showing another aircraft mid-transmission, with the pilot's hand resting, not pressing.

### Card 3 — The word will not come

**Objective ID:** `s1-radio.standard-call`

**Question:** Mid-call, the standard phrase you wanted has gone out of your head. What now?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-2`) Stop transmitting and try again in a few minutes
- **B.** (`opt-1`) Use any phrase that sounds like radio language, even if it is not what you mean
- **C.** (`opt-4`) Say what you need in plain, clear words
- **D.** (`opt-3`) Switch the radio off and stay clear of the airspace

**Correct answer:** `opt-4`

**Explanation:** Standard phraseology exists because it is compact and unambiguous, and it is worth learning — but being understood is the point of the whole exercise. A controller would far rather hear plain words than a phrase used incorrectly. Everybody sounded rough at the start, including every controller you will talk to. Confirm in the Aeronautical Information Manual.

**Visual:** Two speech bubbles of the same size: one dense with standard phraseology, one in plain words, both arriving at a controller who understands both. Neither is drawn as the wrong answer.

## Objective 2 — `s1-radio.nontowered-ctaf`

> Describe operating at a nontowered airport using the CTAF

### Card 4 — Who is in charge at a nontowered field

**Objective ID:** `s1-radio.nontowered-ctaf`

**Question:** At a nontowered airport, who clears you to land?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-3`) The nearest tower, on its own frequency
- **B.** (`opt-1`) Nobody — you announce your intentions and separate yourself from other traffic
- **C.** (`opt-4`) The first aircraft that announced in the pattern
- **D.** (`opt-2`) Whoever answers your call on the CTAF

**Correct answer:** `opt-1`

**Explanation:** There is no clearance at a nontowered field because there is nobody to issue one. Everyone announces on the common frequency, everyone listens, and everyone separates themselves by seeing and being seen. Somebody answering your call is a courtesy and useful information; it is not permission, and nobody there can give you any. Confirm in the Aeronautical Information Manual.

**Visual:** A nontowered field drawn with three aircraft in the pattern, each with a speech bubble containing its own position. No control tower in the picture, and no arrows of instruction between aircraft — only eyes drawn looking outward.

### Card 5 — Saying the airport's name twice

**Objective ID:** `s1-radio.nontowered-ctaf`

**Question:** Self-announce calls at a nontowered field begin and end with the airport's name. Why the repetition?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-4`) Because it is how a controller knows to answer you
- **B.** (`opt-1`) Because the regulation requires the name twice
- **C.** (`opt-3`) Because radios cut off the first word of every transmission
- **D.** (`opt-2`) Because several airports may share the frequency, and the last word tells everyone which field you mean

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** Common frequencies are shared between airports, sometimes ones quite close together, so a pilot hearing "turning base" needs to know which base, at which field. Saying the name at the front and the back means anyone who tuned in late still knows whether the call concerns them. Confirm in the Aeronautical Information Manual.

**Visual:** Two airports drawn some distance apart, both tuned to the same frequency, with one call travelling to both. The call's first and last words are highlighted, and at the second airport a pilot is shown recognising that the call is not about them.

### Card 6 — The airplane you cannot hear

**Objective ID:** `s1-radio.nontowered-ctaf`

**Question:** You have announced every leg and nobody else is on the frequency. What can you conclude?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-3`) That you have the pattern to yourself
- **B.** (`opt-4`) Nothing about traffic — an aircraft may have no radio, a wrong frequency, or a radio that has failed
- **C.** (`opt-1`) That any other traffic must have landed
- **D.** (`opt-2`) That the field is closed

**Correct answer:** `opt-4`

**Explanation:** A radio is not a traffic detector. Aircraft may legally operate at many nontowered fields without one, a radio can fail, and a pilot can be on the wrong frequency believing they are on the right one. The radio adds to your picture; your eyes are still what keep you apart from other aircraft. Confirm in the Aeronautical Information Manual and 14 CFR.

**Visual:** A pattern with your airplane and its speech bubble, and a second aircraft drawn in the same pattern with no bubble at all — present, legal, and silent.

## Objective 3 — `s1-radio.readback-errors`

> Explain what to do after a readback error or a missed call

### Card 7 — You missed it

**Objective ID:** `s1-radio.readback-errors`

**Question:** The controller said something and you did not catch it. What do you do?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-4`) Ask them to say again
- **B.** (`opt-2`) Stay quiet and work out what it probably was
- **C.** (`opt-1`) Acknowledge it so you do not sound lost
- **D.** (`opt-3`) Do nothing, and wait for them to repeat it themselves

**Correct answer:** `opt-4`

**Explanation:** Asking is the shortest way through and costs a few seconds of frequency time. Acknowledging something you did not hear puts a controller under the impression you are about to do a thing you do not know about, which is far more expensive than asking. Confirm in the Aeronautical Information Manual.

**Visual:** A broken speech bubble arriving in the cockpit with a gap in the middle, and a short reply going back containing only the request to repeat. The controller's next bubble is drawn whole.

### Card 8 — You are not certain you were cleared

**Objective ID:** `s1-radio.readback-errors`

**Question:** You think you heard a clearance to cross a runway, but you are not sure it was for you. What do you do?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-3`) Cross, since you probably heard it right
- **B.** (`opt-2`) Cross quickly, to clear the area sooner
- **C.** (`opt-1`) Hold where you are and ask
- **D.** (`opt-4`) Wait silently until someone tells you again

**Correct answer:** `opt-1`

**Explanation:** Runway crossings and hold-short instructions are read back for exactly this reason, and an unresolved doubt about one is a reason to stop moving, not to hurry. Controllers work with student pilots constantly; being asked to confirm a crossing is unremarkable to them. A runway incursion is not. Confirm in the Aeronautical Information Manual.

**Visual:** An airplane stopped short of a hold-short line with a question bubble going up, and the runway beyond drawn plainly. No collision imagery — the stopped airplane and the unanswered question carry it.

### Card 9 — You got it wrong on the air

**Objective ID:** `s1-radio.readback-errors`

**Question:** You read back an instruction incorrectly and the controller corrects you. What is the right response?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-3`) Read back the correction and carry on with the flight
- **B.** (`opt-1`) Apologise at length so they know you are taking it seriously
- **C.** (`opt-4`) Say nothing further, to keep the frequency clear
- **D.** (`opt-2`) Leave the frequency and return once you have composed yourself

**Correct answer:** `opt-3`

**Explanation:** A corrected readback is the system working exactly as designed — that is why readbacks exist. Confirm the correct version so the controller knows you have it, and fly on. An apology takes frequency time that the next aircraft needs, and nobody on the other end is keeping score of how you sounded. Confirm in the Aeronautical Information Manual.

**Visual:** Three short exchanges in sequence: a readback with one word wrong, a correction, and a clean readback. The third is drawn no differently from the first two — the sequence is ordinary, not a drama.
