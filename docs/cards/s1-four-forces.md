## Objective 1 — `s1-four-forces.four-forces`

> Name the four forces and describe how they act in steady flight

### Card 1 — In steady level flight

**Objective ID:** `s1-four-forces.four-forces`

**Question:** The airplane is in straight and level flight at a constant speed. What is true of the four forces?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-3`) Lift balances weight, and thrust balances drag
- **B.** (`opt-1`) Lift is greater than weight, or the airplane would sink
- **C.** (`opt-4`) Thrust is greater than drag at all times in flight
- **D.** (`opt-2`) Weight disappears once the airplane leaves the ground

**Correct answer:** `opt-3`

**Explanation:** Steady means nothing is changing: the forces are in balance, in pairs. Lift greater than weight is what starts a climb — it is not what sustains level flight. Weight never goes anywhere; the wing is simply carrying it. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** An airplane in level flight with four arrows of equal, paired length — up and down, forward and back. A second faded frame beside it shows the upward arrow longer and the airplane beginning to climb, labelled as a different situation.

### Card 2 — Adding power

**Objective ID:** `s1-four-forces.four-forces`

**Question:** From steady level flight, thrust becomes greater than drag. What happens next?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-2`) Nothing changes until the pilot moves the control column
- **B.** (`opt-1`) Weight increases to compensate
- **C.** (`opt-3`) The airplane immediately rolls
- **D.** (`opt-4`) The airplane accelerates, and drag rises as it does

**Correct answer:** `opt-4`

**Explanation:** Unbalance a pair and something changes. More thrust than drag means acceleration — and as speed builds, drag builds with it, until the two are back in balance at a higher speed or the airplane is climbing instead. What actually happens depends on what the pilot does with pitch as well, which is why power and attitude are taught together. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** A sequence of three frames with the thrust arrow lengthened in the first, the drag arrow growing across the next two, and a speed scale beneath showing the change settling.

### Card 3 — Where drag comes from

**Objective ID:** `s1-four-forces.four-forces`

**Question:** Which statement about drag is true?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) Drag comes only from the air hitting the front of the airplane
- **B.** (`opt-2`) Drag exists only at high speed
- **C.** (`opt-3`) Some drag is the price of producing lift, and some comes from pushing the airframe through the air
- **D.** (`opt-4`) Drag acts upward, opposing lift

**Correct answer:** `opt-3`

**Explanation:** Drag has two families: the part that comes with making lift, and the part that comes from shoving an aircraft-shaped object through the air. They behave differently with speed, which is why there is a speed where total drag is least — and why the slow end of the range is not the efficient end it looks like. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** Two curves on a single speed axis, one falling and one rising, with their sum drawn above and a dip marked where the sum is lowest. No values on either axis.

## Objective 2 — `s1-four-forces.aoa-and-lift`

> Explain how angle of attack relates to lift

### Card 4 — What the angle is measured between

**Objective ID:** `s1-four-forces.aoa-and-lift`

**Question:** Angle of attack is the angle between which two things?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-4`) The nose of the airplane and the horizon
- **B.** (`opt-2`) The wing's chord line and the air coming at it
- **C.** (`opt-1`) The wing and the ground
- **D.** (`opt-3`) The flight path and magnetic north

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** Angle of attack is measured between the wing's chord line and the relative wind — the air actually arriving at the wing. It is not pitch attitude, which is measured against the horizon, and the two can differ a great deal: an airplane descending steeply can have a low nose and a high angle of attack at the same time. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** A wing section with the chord line drawn through it and the relative wind arriving at an angle, the angle between them shaded. Beside it, the horizon drawn separately with the pitch angle marked in a different shade, so the two angles are visibly different quantities.

### Card 5 — More angle, more lift — up to a point

**Objective ID:** `s1-four-forces.aoa-and-lift`

**Question:** As the angle of attack increases from small to large, what happens to lift?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) It rises until the critical angle is reached, then falls away
- **B.** (`opt-3`) It rises steadily and keeps rising, however far the angle goes
- **C.** (`opt-4`) It stays the same, since lift depends only on speed
- **D.** (`opt-2`) It falls at first and then rises

**Correct answer:** `opt-1`

**Explanation:** Increasing the angle of attack increases lift — until the air can no longer follow the curve of the upper surface. Past that **critical angle**, the flow separates, lift drops off, and drag climbs sharply. Everything about stalls hangs on that one turning point. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** A single curve of lift against angle of attack, rising to a peak and falling away after it, with the peak marked as the critical angle. Small wing sections along the curve show the airflow attached, then separating past the peak. No numbers on the axes.

**CFI review:** ______________________________________________

### Card 6 — Holding altitude as you slow down

**Objective ID:** `s1-four-forces.aoa-and-lift`

**Question:** You reduce power and slow down while holding your altitude. What must happen to the angle of attack?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-2`) It must decrease, because there is less power
- **B.** (`opt-1`) It stays the same, and the airplane descends instead
- **C.** (`opt-3`) It has nothing to do with speed
- **D.** (`opt-4`) It must increase, to keep producing the lift that carries the weight

**Correct answer:** `opt-4`

**Explanation:** The wing still has to carry the same weight. Slower air over the wing produces less lift at a given angle, so the angle has to increase to make up the difference — which is why slow flight is flown with the nose noticeably higher. It is also why slowing down while holding altitude walks the wing steadily toward its critical angle. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge and the Airplane Flying Handbook.

**Visual:** Three frames at the same altitude, speed decreasing left to right, with the shaded angle of attack growing in each and the lift arrow staying exactly the same length throughout.

## Objective 3 — `s1-four-forces.stall-is-aoa`

> Describe a stall in terms of angle of attack rather than airspeed

### Card 7 — What the published stall speed assumes

**Objective ID:** `s1-four-forces.stall-is-aoa`

**Question:** Why is the stall speed in the handbook true only under particular conditions?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-3`) Because the airspeed indicator is inaccurate at low speeds
- **B.** (`opt-2`) Because the critical angle of attack changes with the weather
- **C.** (`opt-1`) Because it is measured for a particular weight, configuration, and manoeuvre — while the critical angle of attack itself does not change
- **D.** (`opt-4`) Because stall speed varies with the fuel grade used

**Correct answer:** `opt-1`

**Explanation:** A published stall speed is the speed at which that airplane, loaded and configured a particular way, reaches its critical angle of attack in a particular manoeuvre. Change the weight, the flap setting, or the load you are pulling, and the speed changes — the angle does not. That is why the angle is the reliable idea and the speed is the shorthand. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge and the POH or AFM for your airplane.

**Visual:** One wing at its critical angle drawn three times, each in a different situation — light, heavy, and in a turn — with a different airspeed label beneath each and the identical shaded angle in all three.

### Card 8 — What is happening to the air

**Objective ID:** `s1-four-forces.stall-is-aoa`

**Question:** Past the critical angle of attack, what is happening over the wing?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-4`) The air becomes too thin to support the airplane
- **B.** (`opt-2`) The airflow separates from the upper surface, so lift falls off and drag rises
- **C.** (`opt-1`) The wing stops moving through the air
- **D.** (`opt-3`) The air flows faster underneath than over the top for the first time

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** Below the critical angle the air follows the curve of the upper surface. Past it, the flow breaks away into turbulence, the lift the wing was producing falls off, and drag increases sharply. The airplane is still moving through perfectly ordinary air — what changed is the angle at which the wing is meeting it. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** Two wing sections, one with smooth streamlines following the upper surface, one past the critical angle with the flow breaking into eddies behind the separation point. Both drawn at the same airspeed, labelled as such.

### Card 9 — The same airplane, heavier

**Objective ID:** `s1-four-forces.stall-is-aoa`

**Question:** Two identical trainers, one loaded much heavier than the other. What differs about their stalls?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) The heavier one stalls at a larger angle of attack
- **B.** (`opt-4`) The heavier one cannot stall at all
- **C.** (`opt-2`) They stall at exactly the same airspeed, since they are the same model
- **D.** (`opt-3`) The heavier one reaches the critical angle at a higher airspeed; the angle itself is the same for both

**Correct answer:** `opt-3`

**Explanation:** More weight needs more lift, and at a given angle of attack more lift needs more speed — so the heavier airplane arrives at its critical angle while still going faster. The critical angle belongs to the wing and does not move. This is also why a stall speed quoted by another pilot is worth nothing until you know how their airplane was loaded. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** Two identical airplanes side by side with different weight arrows, each at the same shaded critical angle, with a speed scale beneath showing the heavier one further to the right.
