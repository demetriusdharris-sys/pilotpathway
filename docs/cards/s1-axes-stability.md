## Objective 1 — `s1-axes-stability.three-axes`

> Describe motion about the lateral, longitudinal, and vertical axes

### Card 1 — Rolling

**Objective ID:** `s1-axes-stability.three-axes`

**Question:** The airplane rolls. Which axis is it moving about?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) The lateral axis, which runs wingtip to wingtip
- **B.** (`opt-4`) The vertical axis, which runs top to bottom
- **C.** (`opt-2`) None — rolling is a movement of the wings, not of the airplane
- **D.** (`opt-3`) The longitudinal axis, which runs nose to tail

**Correct answer:** `opt-3`

**Explanation:** Roll happens about the longitudinal axis, the line from nose to tail. Pitch is about the lateral axis, wingtip to wingtip, and yaw is about the vertical axis. All three pass through the airplane's centre of gravity, which is why loading it differently changes how it behaves. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** An airplane drawn with three rods through its centre of gravity in three different shades, and a small curved arrow around the nose-to-tail rod only.

### Card 2 — Where the axes meet

**Objective ID:** `s1-axes-stability.three-axes`

**Question:** Where do the three axes intersect?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-4`) At the nose
- **B.** (`opt-2`) At the centre of gravity
- **C.** (`opt-1`) At the tail
- **D.** (`opt-3`) At the point where the wings meet the fuselage

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** All three pass through the centre of gravity, and the airplane rotates about that point — which is why where the weight sits changes how the airplane handles. Load it outside the approved range and the behaviour you have been taught to expect stops being the behaviour you get. The limits are in your airplane's handbook: `[CFI: confirm value]`. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge and the POH or AFM for your airplane.

### Card 3 — Two at once

**Objective ID:** `s1-axes-stability.three-axes`

**Question:** In a normal turn, how many axes is the airplane moving about?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) One — only roll
- **B.** (`opt-4`) One — only yaw, since the turn changes heading
- **C.** (`opt-3`) More than one — the airplane rolls, yaws, and needs pitch to hold altitude
- **D.** (`opt-2`) None, once the bank is established

**Correct answer:** `opt-3`

**Explanation:** A turn is not a single-axis manoeuvre. You roll into it, the nose yaws as the turn develops, and back pressure holds the altitude because the lift that was carrying the weight is now partly pulling you round the turn. Seeing the three as things that happen together, rather than as separate lessons, is what makes a coordinated turn make sense. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge and the Airplane Flying Handbook.

**Visual:** A turn drawn in three small panels — entry, established, rollout — with the axes involved highlighted in each, more than one lit in every panel.

## Objective 2 — `s1-axes-stability.stability-vs-control`

> Explain the difference between stability and controllability

### Card 4 — What stability means

**Objective ID:** `s1-axes-stability.stability-vs-control`

**Question:** A gust displaces the nose and the airplane returns toward where it was without your input. What is that?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-2`) Stability — the airplane's tendency to return to its original condition
- **B.** (`opt-4`) Controllability — the airplane responding to the controls
- **C.** (`opt-1`) A trim failure
- **D.** (`opt-3`) A stall beginning

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** Stability is what the airplane does on its own after it is disturbed. A stable airplane heads back toward where it was, which is restful on a long flight and forgiving of a student's attention wandering. Controllability is a different question: how readily it does what you ask. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** A ball resting in a shallow bowl, nudged and rolling back toward the centre, drawn beside an airplane's nose displaced by a gust and returning. No control inputs drawn in either.

### Card 5 — The trade

**Objective ID:** `s1-axes-stability.stability-vs-control`

**Question:** Why is a trainer not designed to be as manoeuvrable as an aerobatic airplane?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) Because manoeuvrability is dangerous in all circumstances
- **B.** (`opt-4`) Because trainers have less powerful engines
- **C.** (`opt-2`) Because the regulations forbid manoeuvrable light aircraft
- **D.** (`opt-3`) Because stability and manoeuvrability pull against each other, and a trainer is deliberately built toward the stable end

**Correct answer:** `opt-3`

**Explanation:** The more strongly an airplane resists being displaced, the more effort it takes to displace it on purpose — so designers choose a point on that scale for the job the airplane does. A trainer sits toward stable, which is why it tends to fly itself straight while you are looking at a chart. That is a design choice in your favour, not a shortcoming. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** A single slider between two labelled ends, stable and manoeuvrable, with a trainer marked toward one end and an aerobatic airplane toward the other. No point on the slider is marked as better.

### Card 6 — What trim is doing

**Objective ID:** `s1-axes-stability.stability-vs-control`

**Question:** What does trimming the airplane achieve?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-2`) It locks the controls in position
- **B.** (`opt-4`) It relieves the control pressure you are holding, so the airplane maintains the attitude without you pushing or pulling
- **C.** (`opt-1`) It increases the airplane's maximum speed
- **D.** (`opt-3`) It replaces the need to use the controls at all

**Correct answer:** `opt-4`

**Explanation:** Trim takes the steady pressure off your hand so you can fly with your fingertips instead of your forearm, and it makes the airplane's own stability work for you. It does not lock anything, and an airplane in trim still has to be flown — a change in power or configuration means trimming again. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge and the Airplane Flying Handbook.

**Visual:** Two frames of the same airplane holding the same attitude, one with a hand gripping the control and a pressure arrow, one with the hand resting lightly and no arrow. The trim wheel is shaded in the second.

## Objective 3 — `s1-axes-stability.adverse-yaw`

> Describe what adverse yaw is and how it is corrected

### Card 7 — The correction

**Objective ID:** `s1-axes-stability.adverse-yaw`

**Question:** How does a pilot correct for adverse yaw?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) Opposite rudder, away from the turn
- **B.** (`opt-4`) Back pressure on the control column
- **C.** (`opt-3`) Rudder applied with the aileron, in the direction of the turn
- **D.** (`opt-2`) More power as the turn begins

**Correct answer:** `opt-3`

**Explanation:** Rudder goes in with the aileron and in the same direction as the turn, which cancels the yaw the ailerons produce. Your instructor will have you feel this rather than calculate it — how much and for how long depends on the airplane and on how quickly you are rolling. Confirm in the Airplane Flying Handbook and the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** A plan view of a roll into a left turn with the aileron deflection and the rudder deflection drawn together, and the nose tracking the intended path rather than swinging wide of it.

### Card 8 — Designed out, partly

**Objective ID:** `s1-axes-stability.adverse-yaw`

**Question:** Why do many airplanes still show adverse yaw even though designers know about it?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-2`) Because design features reduce it rather than removing it, so some is left for the pilot to handle
- **B.** (`opt-4`) Because it only appears in airplanes built before a certain date
- **C.** (`opt-1`) Because pilots would lose the skill if it were designed out
- **D.** (`opt-3`) Because removing it would make the airplane unstable

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** Designers use several tricks — moving the up-going and down-going ailerons by different amounts, shaping them so the up-going one adds drag — and these reduce adverse yaw rather than abolishing it. Which features your airplane has, and how much is left over, is a matter for your airplane and your feet. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** Two aileron sections drawn in cross-section, one deflected up further than the other is deflected down, with the drag arrows sketched at each. A small residual yaw arrow remains at the nose.

### Card 9 — When you notice it most

**Objective ID:** `s1-axes-stability.adverse-yaw`

**Question:** When is adverse yaw most noticeable?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-3`) At high speed in level flight
- **B.** (`opt-4`) Only on the ground during taxi
- **C.** (`opt-2`) Only in descent
- **D.** (`opt-1`) At slow speed with large aileron inputs

**Correct answer:** `opt-1`

**Explanation:** Slow flight with big control movements is where it shows up most — the rudder is less effective at low speed, and larger aileron deflections produce more of the drag difference that causes the yaw in the first place. That is also the part of the envelope where being sloppy with your feet costs the most. Confirm in the Airplane Flying Handbook.

**Visual:** Two panels of the same roll input, one at cruise speed with a small yaw arrow and one in slow flight with a noticeably larger one, drawn at the same scale.
