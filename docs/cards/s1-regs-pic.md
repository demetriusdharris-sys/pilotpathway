# Quiz cards for review — Stage 1, "You are PIC — even as a student"

**Lesson:** `s1-regs-pic` · **ACS area of operation:** Preflight Preparation · **Sources drawn on:** 14 CFR, AIM

**Status: DRAFT. Not shown to any student until a CFI signs off.**

## What we are asking you to do

Nine cards, three for each of the lesson's three learning objectives. Two of the three objectives are marked safety-critical, and the lesson is where a student first meets the idea that the decision is theirs.

Please check three things:

1. **Is it accurate?** Regulations especially. If a card states something the regulation does not say, or says it in a way that would mislead a student in an oral exam, mark it.
2. **Are the wrong answers realistic?** Each wrong option is meant to be a belief a real student pilot holds. If one is obviously silly, the card teaches elimination rather than flying.
3. **What should be cut?** Nine cards for one lesson may be too many. Say which earn their place.

Two things you will see in the text, deliberately:

- **`[CFI: confirm value]`** — a number or a limit that is aircraft-specific, or that we were not certain of. We left a gap rather than guess.
- **`FLAG FOR CFI:`** — we have a specific doubt about that card and have written down what it is.

No section numbers, ACS task codes, or figure numbers appear anywhere. Sources are named only — a student who quotes a stale section number to a DPE pays for our mistake.

## About the A–D letters

**The options are shuffled every time a card is shown to a student.** The letters here are display only, so that we can talk about the options in this document. Each option also carries a stable id, `opt-1` to `opt-4`, and **Correct answer** names that id rather than a letter.

Two things follow, and they matter if you rewrite an option: never write an option that refers to another by letter, and there is no "all of the above" or "none of the above" — both depend on there being an above.

The ids do not run in alphabetical order down a card. An option keeps its id when the options are reordered, so seeing **A.** (`opt-3`) is expected, not a typo.

There is a blank **CFI review** line at the end of every card.

## Objective 1 — `s1-regs-pic.pic-authority`

> Describe the authority and responsibility of the pilot in command

### Card 1 — Whose decision it is

**Objective ID:** `s1-regs-pic.pic-authority`

**Question:** Your instructor has endorsed you for solo flight. Today the wind is within every limit written on your endorsement, but stronger than anything you have handled alone. Who decides whether you fly?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) Your instructor, because the endorsement already settled it
- **B.** (`opt-4`) The tower, since they clear you for takeoff
- **C.** (`opt-3`) You do — as pilot in command you are the final authority for that flight
- **D.** (`opt-2`) Nobody — if it is inside the limits on the endorsement, the flight is required to go

**Correct answer:** `opt-3`

**Explanation:** 14 CFR makes the pilot in command directly responsible for, and the final authority as to, the operation of the aircraft. An endorsement sets an outer boundary; it does not oblige you to fly to it. **Legal and safe are two different questions**, and only one person answers the second one on the day. Confirm in 14 CFR and the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** A single cockpit seat drawn from behind, with three arrows pointing toward it labelled "instructor", "tower", "schedule" — and one arrow pointing outward from the seat to the runway, drawn heavier than the other three.

### Card 2 — Deviating in an emergency

**Objective ID:** `s1-regs-pic.pic-authority`

**Question:** An in-flight emergency needs immediate action, and the only safe course would break a rule. What do the regulations allow?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) You may deviate to the extent required to meet that emergency, and may be asked afterwards for a written report
- **B.** (`opt-2`) You must hold to every rule and manage the emergency within them
- **C.** (`opt-4`) You may deviate only if ATC approves the deviation first
- **D.** (`opt-3`) You may deviate freely, and nothing is ever asked about it afterwards

**Correct answer:** `opt-1`

**Explanation:** 14 CFR allows the pilot in command to deviate from the rules to the extent required to meet an in-flight emergency that demands immediate action, and the Administrator may request a written report afterwards. The authority is real, it is bounded by what the emergency actually requires, and it is accountable after the fact. Confirm in 14 CFR.

**Visual:** A rule book drawn open, with a single page lifted aside just far enough for an airplane silhouette to pass through — the rest of the book still closed. A small form icon on the far side, captioned "afterwards, on request."

### Card 3 — Signed off does not mean checked

**Objective ID:** `s1-regs-pic.pic-authority`

**Question:** The maintenance log shows the airplane was signed off this morning, and the school's dispatcher released it to you. On your walkaround you find something that does not look right. What is your responsibility?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-4`) Fly it — the signature is what makes it airworthy
- **B.** (`opt-1`) Ask the dispatcher to decide, since they released it
- **C.** (`opt-3`) Fly the pattern once to see whether it matters
- **D.** (`opt-2`) You decide whether the aircraft is in condition for safe flight, and you do not go if it is not

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** Maintenance and the operator have their own responsibilities, but 14 CFR puts the determination that the aircraft is in condition for safe flight on the pilot in command. Nobody else is in the airplane when the decision matters. Saying "it is not going today" is a normal thing for a student to say, and a CFI would rather hear it than not. Confirm in 14 CFR and the Airplane Flying Handbook.

**Visual:** Two panels. Left, a logbook page with a signature. Right, a gloved hand at the airplane holding an item at eye level. An arrow runs from left to right and stops short of the airplane, with a small gap drawn deliberately between them.

## Objective 2 — `s1-regs-pic.student-limitations`

> Describe student pilot privileges and limitations

### Card 4 — Someone asks to come along

**Objective ID:** `s1-regs-pic.student-limitations`

**Question:** Your cousin asks to ride along on your next solo flight, just to watch. What do the regulations say?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-3`) It is allowed if they are family
- **B.** (`opt-1`) A student pilot may not carry passengers
- **C.** (`opt-2`) It is allowed if your instructor says it is fine
- **D.** (`opt-4`) It is allowed as long as nobody pays anything

**Correct answer:** `opt-1`

**Explanation:** 14 CFR does not permit a student pilot to carry passengers, and no endorsement or permission changes that — it is a limit on the certificate itself. Solo means solo. The limitation ends when you hold a certificate that allows passengers, which is one of the things the private pilot certificate buys you. Confirm in 14 CFR.

**Visual:** A two-seat cockpit drawn from above with the right seat empty and the seatbelt fastened across it, as it would be for solo flight. No crossed-out figure, no scolding — just the empty seat.

### Card 5 — Gas money

**Objective ID:** `s1-regs-pic.student-limitations`

**Question:** A neighbour offers to cover your fuel if you fly a box of parts to an airport an hour away. Can you do it as a student pilot?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-4`) Yes, if you would have made the flight anyway
- **B.** (`opt-1`) Yes, as long as the payment only covers fuel and not your time
- **C.** (`opt-2`) No — a student pilot may not carry property for compensation or hire, and may not act as pilot in command for compensation
- **D.** (`opt-3`) Yes, if the parts belong to the flight school

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** 14 CFR bars a student pilot from carrying property for compensation or hire and from acting as pilot in command for compensation or hire. Covering costs still counts as compensation, and "I was going anyway" does not change what the flight is. This is one of the first places where a favour and a commercial operation look the same from the outside. Confirm in 14 CFR.

**Visual:** A cardboard box on a passenger seat with a fuel receipt resting on top of it, drawn plainly. Beside it, a small scale, with the box on one side and the receipt on the other, balancing level.

**CFI review:** ______________________________________________

### Card 6 — What has to be true before you fly solo

**Objective ID:** `s1-regs-pic.student-limitations`

**Question:** What must be in place before a student pilot flies solo?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-3`) Enough total flight hours logged
- **B.** (`opt-1`) A passing grade on the knowledge test
- **C.** (`opt-2`) A medical certificate on its own
- **D.** (`opt-4`) A current instructor endorsement for solo flight in that make and model, and endorsements covering the flight you are about to make

**Correct answer:** `opt-4`

**Explanation:** Solo flight rests on your instructor's endorsements — for solo in that make and model, kept current, plus the specific endorsements a particular flight needs, such as a solo cross-country. Hours, the knowledge test, and a medical all have their place, but none of them is what authorises you to fly alone. This is also why an AI cannot endorse anyone: **the endorsement is a certificated instructor putting their certificate behind your readiness.** Confirm in 14 CFR and with your CFI.

**Visual:** A logbook page drawn open with an endorsement block and a signature, and a date circled. Beside it, a calendar with a currency window shaded, showing that the endorsement has a life span rather than being permanent.

**FLAG FOR CFI:** We deliberately did not list the solo visibility and surface-reference limits on this card, to keep it to one idea and to avoid putting numbers in front of a student we cannot be certain they will read correctly. Please tell us whether that belongs here as a fourth card instead, or whether leaving it out makes this card incomplete.

## Objective 3 — `s1-regs-pic.required-documents`

> Identify the documents required aboard the aircraft and on your person

### Card 7 — What lives in the airplane

**Objective ID:** `s1-regs-pic.required-documents`

**Question:** Which set of documents belongs in the aircraft itself?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-2`) Airworthiness certificate, registration, operating limitations, and weight and balance data
- **B.** (`opt-4`) Your pilot certificate, your medical, and your logbook
- **C.** (`opt-1`) The maintenance records for the last year
- **D.** (`opt-3`) The instructor's certificate and the school's operating certificate

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** The aircraft carries its own paperwork: the airworthiness certificate, the registration, the operating limitations (the approved flight manual or placards), and weight and balance information. Maintenance records are kept, but not normally carried in the airplane. Many pilots remember the set with a mnemonic; the point of the card is the contents, not the letters. Confirm in 14 CFR and the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** A document pocket in the airplane drawn open, with four labelled edges showing. Separately, on the pilot's lap, a wallet — the two kept visually apart to make the point that the two sets are different.

**FLAG FOR CFI:** The radio station licence is deliberately left off this card, since it is not required for domestic flight in a trainer. Please confirm you want it left out at this stage rather than mentioned as an international exception.

### Card 8 — What lives on you

**Objective ID:** `s1-regs-pic.required-documents`

**Question:** You are walking out to fly solo. What must you have with you personally?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-3`) Your logbook and nothing else
- **B.** (`opt-1`) Your student pilot certificate, your medical certificate, and government-issued photo identification
- **C.** (`opt-2`) Your photo identification only
- **D.** (`opt-4`) Nothing, as long as the aircraft documents are aboard

**Correct answer:** `opt-1`

**Explanation:** For solo flight in an airplane you carry your student pilot certificate, an appropriate medical certificate, and photo identification. Your logbook matters too — it holds the endorsements that make the flight legal — and your instructor will tell you when it must be with you. Confirm in 14 CFR and with your CFI.

**Visual:** Three cards laid flat side by side, drawn at the same size and with no personal details filled in. Beneath them, a logbook drawn slightly apart, labelled "where the endorsements live."

**FLAG FOR CFI:** We have written "an appropriate medical certificate" rather than naming a class, because the alternatives available to a student are not all the same and we did not want to state one as the rule. Please tell us how you would like this phrased for a first-time reader.

### Card 9 — Where the airworthiness certificate goes

**Objective ID:** `s1-regs-pic.required-documents`

**Question:** Why is the airworthiness certificate displayed in the cabin rather than filed in a folder?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-2`) Because it must be displayed where it is legible to passengers and crew
- **B.** (`opt-4`) So it does not get lost among the other paperwork
- **C.** (`opt-1`) Because it has to be signed before every flight
- **D.** (`opt-3`) Because it expires and needs checking on each flight

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** 14 CFR requires the airworthiness certificate to be displayed so that it is legible to passengers and crew — which is why you see it near the cabin entrance rather than in a folder. It does not need a signature before each flight, and it stays valid while the aircraft is maintained as required. Confirm in 14 CFR.

**Visual:** The cabin doorway of a trainer, with the certificate mounted flat and legible at eye height, drawn from the point of view of someone stepping in.
