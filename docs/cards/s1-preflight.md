# Quiz cards for review — Stage 1, "Preflight like it matters"

**Lesson:** `s1-preflight` · **ACS area of operation:** Preflight Procedures · **Sources drawn on:** AFH, PHAK

**Status: DRAFT. Not shown to any student until a CFI signs off.**

## What we are asking you to do

Nine cards, three for each of the lesson's three learning objectives. None of the three is marked safety-critical in our system, which we think understates this lesson — the habits it builds are the ones a student carries into every flight afterwards. Tell us if you think the flags should change.

Please check three things:

1. **Is it accurate?** Especially where a card describes a procedure that varies between schools and airplanes. We have tried to keep "the rule" and "the technique" apart; say where we have not.
2. **Are the wrong answers realistic?** Each wrong option is meant to be a shortcut a real student takes, not a silly answer.
3. **What should be cut?** Say which cards earn their place.

Two things you will see in the text, deliberately:

- **`[CFI: confirm value]`** — a number or limit that is aircraft-specific, or that we were not certain of. We left a gap rather than guess.
- **`FLAG FOR CFI:`** — we have a specific doubt about that card and have written down what it is.

No section numbers, ACS task codes, or figure numbers appear anywhere. Sources are named only.

## About the A–D letters

**The options are shuffled every time a card is shown to a student.** The letters here are display only. Each option carries a stable id, `opt-1` to `opt-4`, and **Correct answer** names that id rather than a letter. Never write an option that refers to another by letter, and there is no "all of the above" or "none of the above".

The ids do not run in alphabetical order down a card. An option keeps its id when the options are reordered.

There is a blank **CFI review** line at the end of every card.

## Objective 1 — `s1-preflight.airworthiness`

> Determine whether an aircraft is airworthy before flight

### Card 1 — What airworthy actually means

**Objective ID:** `s1-preflight.airworthiness`

**Question:** What has to be true for an aircraft to be airworthy?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) It has fuel aboard and the engine starts
- **B.** (`opt-4`) Someone signed the maintenance records at some point
- **C.** (`opt-3`) It conforms to its type certificate and approved alterations, and it is in condition for safe flight
- **D.** (`opt-2`) It flew yesterday without any trouble

**Correct answer:** `opt-3`

**Explanation:** Airworthy has two halves, and both must be true: the aircraft matches the design it was certificated to, including any approved changes, **and** it is in condition for safe flight. Flying yesterday tells you about yesterday. The second half is the one you are checking on your walkaround. Confirm in 14 CFR and the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** A balance scale with two pans, one labelled "matches its approved design" and one labelled "in condition for safe flight", drawn level. An airplane silhouette sits above the pivot, supported only when both pans are down.

### Card 2 — Where you find out

**Objective ID:** `s1-preflight.airworthiness`

**Question:** How do you find out whether the inspections the aircraft requires are current?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-4`) Check the aircraft's maintenance records
- **B.** (`opt-2`) Check the expiry date on the airworthiness certificate
- **C.** (`opt-1`) Assume the flight school would not release an aircraft that was out of date
- **D.** (`opt-3`) Look for a placard in the cockpit

**Correct answer:** `opt-4`

**Explanation:** Inspection status lives in the maintenance records, and checking it is part of the pilot in command's job before flight. The airworthiness certificate itself does not carry an expiry date the way a licence does — it stays valid while the aircraft is maintained as required. Your instructor will show you which records to look at and what to look for. Confirm in 14 CFR and with your CFI.

**Visual:** Two open logbooks — airframe and engine — with a hand resting on an entry and a date. Beside them, the airworthiness certificate drawn with no expiry field, so the absence is visible.

### Card 3 — Something is broken

**Objective ID:** `s1-preflight.airworthiness`

**Question:** On your walkaround you find a piece of equipment that does not work. What decides whether the flight can legally go?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-3`) Whether you personally think you need it today
- **B.** (`opt-2`) Whether it worked on the last flight
- **C.** (`opt-4`) Whether the airplane will still fly without it
- **D.** (`opt-1`) Whether the item is required for this aircraft and this flight, and whether it has been dealt with the way the rules require

**Correct answer:** `opt-1`

**Explanation:** Inoperative equipment is not automatically a no-go, and it is not automatically fine either. What matters is whether that item is required — by the regulations, by the aircraft's equipment list, and by the kind of flight you are making — and whether it has been handled the way the rules require before the aircraft flies. This is a conversation with your instructor and the maintenance staff, not a judgment call at the airplane. Confirm in 14 CFR and with your CFI.

**Visual:** A single instrument drawn dark and unlit in an otherwise normal panel. Beside the panel, three small documents fanned out and labelled "regulations", "equipment list", "this flight" — the three things consulted before the airplane moves.

**FLAG FOR CFI:** We deliberately kept this general rather than naming the specific path a trainer follows, because schools differ and we did not want to teach one school's procedure as the rule. Please tell us how you would have a pre-solo student think about this, and whether this card should name the steps or stay as a "go ask" card.

## Objective 2 — `s1-preflight.walkaround-flow`

> Perform a systematic preflight inspection using the checklist

### Card 4 — Why the checklist is in your hand

**Objective ID:** `s1-preflight.walkaround-flow`

**Question:** You have done the walkaround thirty times and know it by heart. Why keep the checklist in your hand?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-2`) Because checklists are only for students and checkrides
- **B.** (`opt-4`) Because a checklist does not get tired, distracted, or hurried — and you do
- **C.** (`opt-1`) Because memory is unreliable only for people new to flying
- **D.** (`opt-3`) Because the regulations require you to hold one

**Correct answer:** `opt-4`

**Explanation:** Knowing the flow and using the checklist are not in competition: the flow is how you move around the airplane, and the checklist is how you confirm you missed nothing. The items most often skipped are the ones a pilot is sure they already did. Experienced pilots use checklists more carefully, not less. Confirm in the Airplane Flying Handbook.

**Visual:** A pilot mid-walkaround with the checklist held at chest height, and a faint dotted path around the airplane showing the flow. The checklist and the path are drawn as two separate lines that meet at each inspection point.

### Card 5 — Interrupted halfway

**Objective ID:** `s1-preflight.walkaround-flow`

**Question:** Someone stops you to chat in the middle of your preflight. When you get back to it, what is the safest thing to do?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-3`) Carry on from wherever you think you were
- **B.** (`opt-1`) Skip ahead, since the rest is quicker to check in the air
- **C.** (`opt-4`) Start the engine and check what you missed during the runup
- **D.** (`opt-2`) Go back to a point you are certain about and re-check from there

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** An interruption is one of the most reliable ways to lose an item, because you remember the conversation rather than the last thing you touched. Going back to a known point costs a minute. This is the same habit that later keeps interruptions on the ground from following you into the cockpit. Confirm in the Airplane Flying Handbook and the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** The walkaround path drawn as a loop with a break in it where the interruption happened, and an arrow doubling back to the previous inspection point rather than jumping forward.

### Card 6 — Trusting the gauge

**Objective ID:** `s1-preflight.walkaround-flow`

**Question:** The fuel gauges read enough for your flight. What does the preflight ask of you anyway?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-4`) Check the quantity in the tanks yourself, and sample the fuel for water and contamination
- **B.** (`opt-2`) Nothing further — the gauges are certified instruments
- **C.** (`opt-1`) Add fuel until the tanks are full, every flight, whatever the gauges say
- **D.** (`opt-3`) Run the engine for a while and watch whether the gauge moves

**Correct answer:** `opt-4`

**Explanation:** Fuel gauges in light aircraft are not precision instruments, and pilots have run tanks dry with a needle showing fuel remaining. You look in the tanks, you sample from the drains, and you confirm the fuel is the right grade for your airplane — which is in its handbook, not a general rule. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge and the POH or AFM for your airplane.

**Visual:** Three frames: a fuel gauge needle; a fuel tank opening with a dipstick or visual check; a sample cup held up to the light. The three drawn the same size, so none reads as the shortcut.

## Objective 3 — `s1-preflight.start-taxi-runup`

> Describe proper engine starting, taxi, and runup procedures

### Card 7 — Before the key turns

**Objective ID:** `s1-preflight.start-taxi-runup`

**Question:** What has to happen before you start the engine?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-3`) Set full power so the engine catches quickly
- **B.** (`opt-4`) Release the brakes so the airplane is free to move
- **C.** (`opt-1`) Check that the area around the propeller is clear and call out to warn anyone nearby
- **D.** (`opt-2`) Turn on the landing light to signal the ramp

**Correct answer:** `opt-1`

**Explanation:** A propeller is invisible once it is turning and dangerous before it is. Looking and calling out costs a second and is the one part of the start that protects someone other than you. The rest of the start — throttle setting, mixture, primer — is specific to your airplane and comes from its checklist. Confirm in the Airplane Flying Handbook and the POH or AFM for your airplane.

**Visual:** A view from the cockpit looking out over the nose, with the arc the propeller will occupy drawn as a faint circle extending well beyond the spinner, and a person walking on the ramp inside that circle.

### Card 8 — The first few feet of taxi

**Objective ID:** `s1-preflight.start-taxi-runup`

**Question:** The airplane has just started to roll. What do you check straight away?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-2`) That the radios are tuned correctly
- **B.** (`opt-4`) That the brakes work, while you are still moving slowly and have room
- **C.** (`opt-1`) That the engine gauges are in the green
- **D.** (`opt-3`) Nothing — brakes are checked at the runup

**Correct answer:** `opt-4`

**Explanation:** The brake check belongs in the first few feet of movement, where finding a problem means stopping on the ramp rather than discovering it approaching a hold-short line. Radios and engine gauges matter too, and they have their own place on the checklist. Confirm in the Airplane Flying Handbook.

**Visual:** A taxiway drawn from above with an airplane just clear of its parking spot, the first short segment of the path shaded and labelled "brakes checked here", and a hold-short line drawn far ahead.

### Card 9 — What the runup is for

**Objective ID:** `s1-preflight.start-taxi-runup`

**Question:** Why is the runup done where it is, before you take the runway?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-2`) It is the last place a problem with the engine or controls costs you nothing but a taxi back
- **B.** (`opt-3`) It is a tradition that keeps the engine sounding healthy
- **C.** (`opt-1`) It warms the engine, and nothing else about it matters
- **D.** (`opt-4`) It is required only when the airplane has been sitting for a long time

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** The runup is a test with the airplane still on the ground and still stopped: ignition system, engine instruments, controls free and correct, and whatever else your airplane's checklist calls for. Warming the engine is part of it, not the point of it. A problem found here is an inconvenience; the same problem found on climbout is not.
