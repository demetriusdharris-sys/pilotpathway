# Quiz cards for review — Stage 1, "The traffic pattern"

**Lesson:** `s1-pattern` · **ACS area of operation:** Takeoffs, Landings, and Go-Arounds · **Sources drawn on:** AIM, AFH, 14 CFR

**Status: DRAFT. Not shown to any student until a CFI signs off.**

## What we are asking you to do

Nine cards, three for each of the lesson's three learning objectives. Two of the three objectives are marked safety-critical: right-of-way, and the go-around.

Please check three things:

1. **Is it accurate?** Above all where a card describes what the pattern normally is, as opposed to what a regulation requires. We have tried to keep those two apart; tell us where we have not.
2. **Are the wrong answers realistic?** Each wrong option is meant to be a mistake a student pilot actually makes in the pattern.
3. **What should be cut?** Say which cards earn their place.

Two things you will see in the text, deliberately:

- **`[CFI: confirm value]`** — a number that varies by airport or aircraft, or that we were not certain of. We left a gap rather than guess.
- **`FLAG FOR CFI:`** — we have a specific doubt about that card and have written down what it is.

No section numbers, ACS task codes, or figure numbers appear anywhere. Sources are named only.

## About the A–D letters

**The options are shuffled every time a card is shown to a student.** The letters here are display only. Each option carries a stable id, `opt-1` to `opt-4`, and **Correct answer** names that id rather than a letter. Never write an option that refers to another by letter, and there is no "all of the above" or "none of the above".

The ids do not run in alphabetical order down a card. An option keeps its id when the options are reordered.

There is a blank **CFI review** line at the end of every card.

## Objective 1 — `s1-pattern.pattern-legs`

> Describe a standard traffic pattern and each of its legs

### Card 1 — Naming where you are

**Objective ID:** `s1-pattern.pattern-legs`

**Question:** You have flown past the end of the runway, turned toward it, and are descending — but you are not lined up with it yet. Which leg are you on?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-4`) Final
- **B.** (`opt-2`) Base
- **C.** (`opt-1`) Downwind
- **D.** (`opt-3`) Crosswind

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** Base is the leg between downwind and final, flown roughly at right angles to the runway, and it is where most of the descent and the last look for traffic happen. Final is the leg aligned with the runway. Getting the names right is not vocabulary for its own sake: on the radio, the leg you say is how everyone else builds a picture of where you are. Confirm in the Aeronautical Information Manual and the Airplane Flying Handbook.

**Visual:** A rectangular pattern drawn around a runway with each leg labelled, and a small airplane symbol placed on base. The other legs stay fully drawn and labelled rather than faded, so the whole shape is learned even while one leg is asked about.

### Card 2 — Which way the turns go

**Objective ID:** `s1-pattern.pattern-legs`

**Question:** At an airport with no published exceptions, which way do traffic pattern turns go?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) Whichever direction the wind favours that day
- **B.** (`opt-4`) Right, so the runway stays visible from the left seat
- **C.** (`opt-2`) Either, as long as you announce which one you are flying
- **D.** (`opt-3`) Left, unless right traffic is indicated for that runway

**Correct answer:** `opt-3`

**Explanation:** Turns in the pattern are to the left unless right traffic is published for that runway — and where it is, it is shown in the Chart Supplement, on the sectional, and on the airport's segmented circle. Checking before you arrive is the habit; guessing from the wind is how two aircraft end up circling in opposite directions. Confirm in the Aeronautical Information Manual and 14 CFR.

**Visual:** Two patterns drawn side by side around identical runways, one left and one right, with the direction of flight arrowed. Between them, a segmented circle with traffic pattern indicators, drawn as the thing that tells the two apart.

### Card 3 — The altitude at an airport you have never flown to

**Objective ID:** `s1-pattern.pattern-legs`

**Question:** You are planning a flight to an airport you have not visited. How do you find out its traffic pattern altitude?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-4`) Look it up before you go — the Chart Supplement publishes it for that airport
- **B.** (`opt-1`) Assume it is the same as your home airport
- **C.** (`opt-2`) Ask on the radio when you arrive
- **D.** (`opt-3`) Fly overhead and match whatever traffic is already there

**Correct answer:** `opt-4`

**Explanation:** Pattern altitude is published per airport, and the Chart Supplement is where you look it up while still on the ground. Typical light-aircraft pattern altitude is often around `[CFI: confirm value]` above field elevation, but airports vary, and matching whoever is already there assumes they got it right. ## Objective 2 — `s1-pattern.right-of-way`

> State the basic right-of-way rules that apply in the pattern

### Card 4 — Two of you on final

**Objective ID:** `s1-pattern.right-of-way`

**Question:** Two aircraft are approaching the same runway to land. One is lower than the other. What do the rules say?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-3`) The faster aircraft has the right of way, to keep the flow moving
- **B.** (`opt-4`) The higher aircraft has the right of way, since it can see the other
- **C.** (`opt-1`) The lower aircraft has the right of way, but must not use that to cut in front of or overtake the other
- **D.** (`opt-2`) Whoever announced first on the radio has the right of way

**Correct answer:** `opt-1`

**Explanation:** 14 CFR gives the right of way to the lower aircraft when two are approaching to land — and in the same breath forbids using that rule to cut in front of or overtake the aircraft on final. **A radio call is not a right of way**; it tells people where you are. Confirm in 14 CFR and the Aeronautical Information Manual.

**Visual:** A side view of a final approach with two airplanes at different heights on the same path, the lower one ahead. A dotted line shows the higher one attempting to descend and slot in ahead, with that path drawn as the thing the rule forbids.

### Card 5 — Converging on the way in

**Objective ID:** `s1-pattern.right-of-way`

**Question:** You are converging with another airplane at about the same altitude, and it is off to your right. Neither of you is landing yet. Who gives way?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-2`) You do — when aircraft of the same category converge, the one on the right has the right of way
- **B.** (`opt-4`) The other aircraft, because you saw it first
- **C.** (`opt-1`) Whichever aircraft is slower gives way
- **D.** (`opt-3`) Neither — both turn left

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** When aircraft of the same category are converging at about the same altitude, the aircraft to the other's right has the right of way, so the airplane on your right keeps its course and you give way. Head-on is the different case: there, both aircraft alter course to the right. Seeing the other aircraft first is what lets you act early; it does not change who gives way. Confirm in 14 CFR.

**Visual:** A plan view of two airplanes converging at a shallow angle, with the right-hand one continuing straight and the left-hand one curving behind it. A separate small panel shows the head-on case with both aircraft turning to their own right.

### Card 6 — Not everything in the pattern has an engine

**Objective ID:** `s1-pattern.right-of-way`

**Question:** You are in the pattern in a trainer and converging with a glider. Who has the right of way?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) Your airplane, because it is under power and can react faster
- **B.** (`opt-2`) The aircraft that entered the pattern first
- **C.** (`opt-3`) The aircraft at the lower altitude, whichever that is
- **D.** (`opt-4`) The glider — the rules give way to the less able to manoeuvre

**Correct answer:** `opt-4`

**Explanation:** 14 CFR orders the categories so that the aircraft with the least ability to manoeuvre or go around comes first — a balloon, then a glider, then an airship, with airplanes and rotorcraft giving way to all of them. An aircraft in distress has the right of way over everything. The engine that makes you able to go around is exactly why the rule asks you to be the one who does. Confirm in 14 CFR.

**Visual:** A simple ordered row: balloon, glider, airship, airplane, drawn at the same size with an arrow beneath running from "gives way least" to "gives way most". An aircraft in distress is drawn set apart at the front of the row.

## Objective 3 — `s1-pattern.go-around`

> Explain when and why to go around

### Card 7 — When it is a go-around

**Objective ID:** `s1-pattern.go-around`

**Question:** Which of these calls for a go-around?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-3`) Only a runway that is physically blocked
- **B.** (`opt-1`) Only an instruction from the tower
- **C.** (`opt-2`) Any approach that is not going to end in a safe landing — too high, too fast, not lined up, or a runway you are not sure is clear
- **D.** (`opt-4`) Only a bounced landing

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** A go-around is the answer to any approach that is not working out, whatever is making it not work — energy, alignment, a runway in doubt, an instruction, or a landing that bounces. The Airplane Flying Handbook treats it as a normal manoeuvre that every pilot practises, not an emergency and not a mark against you. Confirm in the Airplane Flying Handbook.

**Visual:** Four small approach sketches in a row — high, fast, off-centreline, and a runway with something still on it — each with the same climbing arrow drawn away from the runway. One response, four causes.

### Card 8 — Deciding early

**Objective ID:** `s1-pattern.go-around`

**Question:** You are high and fast on final and telling yourself you can still salvage it. Why does the Airplane Flying Handbook press you to decide early?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-4`) Because a late go-around has to be reported
- **B.** (`opt-1`) Because the later the decision, the less height, speed, and time you have to make it work
- **C.** (`opt-2`) Because the engine will not produce full power close to the ground
- **D.** (`opt-3`) Because tower will not approve a go-around below a certain height

**Correct answer:** `opt-1`

**Explanation:** The go-around itself does not get harder — your margins do. Deciding while you still have height and options is what makes it routine, and pressing on to "save" an approach is how a landing accident starts. Nobody has to approve it, and a go-around does not need reporting. Confirm in the Airplane Flying Handbook.

**Visual:** One approach path drawn with three decision points marked along it, and beside each a shrinking bar representing the height and time remaining. No crash imagery — the shrinking bar carries the point.

### Card 9 — Flying the go-around

**Objective ID:** `s1-pattern.go-around`

**Question:** You have decided to go around. What happens first?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-2`) Retract the flaps fully, then add power
- **B.** (`opt-1`) Turn away from the runway immediately
- **C.** (`opt-3`) Hold the landing attitude and wait for the airspeed to build
- **D.** (`opt-4`) Add power and pitch for a climb, then reconfigure in stages as the airplane accelerates

**Correct answer:** `opt-4`

**Explanation:** Power and a climbing attitude come first, and the configuration changes follow in stages as the airplane accelerates — flaps and gear on the schedule for your airplane, which is in its handbook, not in a general rule. Dumping all the flaps at once can cost you the lift you are relying on at low speed. The exact sequence and speeds are aircraft-specific: `[CFI: confirm value]`. Confirm in the Airplane Flying Handbook and the POH or AFM for your airplane.

**Visual:** Three frames along a shallow climb away from the runway: throttle forward and nose raised; flaps moving in a first stage; a stabilised climb with the runway offset to one side so it stays in view. Each frame labelled with the action rather than a speed.
