## Objective 1 — `s1-airport-ramp.runway-numbering`

> Determine runway numbers from magnetic heading

### Card 1 — Reading the number

**Objective ID:** `s1-airport-ramp.runway-numbering`

**Question:** You are lined up on Runway 27. Roughly which way are you pointing?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-2`) East
- **B.** (`opt-4`) West — the number is the magnetic heading with the last digit dropped
- **C.** (`opt-1`) North
- **D.** (`opt-3`) It depends on the airport; the numbers are assigned in order

**Correct answer:** `opt-4`

**Explanation:** Runway numbers come from the runway's magnetic heading, rounded to the nearest ten degrees, with the final zero dropped — so Runway 27 points near 270 degrees magnetic, which is west. That is why the numbers mean the same thing at every airport, and why they are worth reading rather than memorising per field. Confirm in the Aeronautical Information Manual and the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** A compass rose with a runway drawn across it, the threshold numbers at each end and the corresponding headings marked around the rose.

### Card 2 — The other end

**Objective ID:** `s1-airport-ramp.runway-numbering`

**Question:** The runway you landed on is Runway 9. What is the number at the opposite end?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-3`) 19
- **B.** (`opt-4`) 9, at both ends
- **C.** (`opt-1`) 27
- **D.** (`opt-2`) 18

**Correct answer:** `opt-1`

**Explanation:** The same strip of pavement serves two opposite directions, so its two numbers differ by the equivalent of 180 degrees — Runway 9 at one end, Runway 27 at the other. Which one is in use depends on the wind, and it changes during the day. Confirm in the Aeronautical Information Manual.

### Card 3 — When the numbers change

**Objective ID:** `s1-airport-ramp.runway-numbering`

**Question:** An airport renumbers its runways. What most likely happened?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-2`) The earth's magnetic field shifted enough that the old numbers no longer matched the magnetic heading
- **B.** (`opt-4`) The runway was resurfaced
- **C.** (`opt-1`) The airport changed owners
- **D.** (`opt-3`) A new control tower was built

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** Magnetic north moves over time, and when the drift is enough that a runway's number no longer matches its magnetic heading, the numbers are repainted and the charts updated. It is a slow business, and it is a good reason to fly with current charts rather than the ones in the back of the airplane. Confirm in the Aeronautical Information Manual.

**Visual:** The same runway drawn twice, decades apart, with the magnetic north arrow shifted slightly between them and the painted number changed accordingly.

## Objective 2 — `s1-airport-ramp.markings-and-signs`

> Interpret common runway and taxiway markings and signs

### Card 4 — Red sign, white letters

**Objective ID:** `s1-airport-ramp.markings-and-signs`

**Question:** You are taxiing and see a red sign with white lettering. What does the colour tell you?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) It points the way to a taxiway
- **B.** (`opt-4`) It tells you which taxiway you are currently on
- **C.** (`opt-2`) It marks a maintenance area
- **D.** (`opt-3`) It is a mandatory instruction — you may not go past it without a clearance

**Correct answer:** `opt-3`

**Explanation:** Red with white lettering means mandatory: an entrance to a runway or an area you may not enter without permission. Signs that tell you where you are and where things lead use yellow and black instead. Learning the colours before the words means you get the meaning at a glance, which is what you need while taxiing. Confirm in the Aeronautical Information Manual.

**Visual:** Three signs drawn side by side at the same size — red with white text, yellow with black text, black with yellow text — with their categories labelled beneath rather than their specific wording.

**CFI review:** ______________________________________________

### Card 5 — The four yellow lines

**Objective ID:** `s1-airport-ramp.markings-and-signs`

**Question:** Across the taxiway ahead are four yellow lines — two solid, two dashed. What are they?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-4`) The edge of the ramp
- **B.** (`opt-2`) The runway holding position marking
- **C.** (`opt-1`) A closed taxiway marking
- **D.** (`opt-3`) A marking showing where to park

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** That pattern marks the holding position for a runway. Approaching from the solid side, you stop before the lines unless you have been cleared past them; from the dashed side you are leaving the runway environment and may cross. **Solid means stop** is the version worth carrying in your head at a hold short line. Confirm in the Aeronautical Information Manual.

**Visual:** The marking drawn across a taxiway with an airplane approaching from the solid side and stopping, and a second airplane crossing from the dashed side, both drawn in the same frame.

### Card 6 — Knowing where you are

**Objective ID:** `s1-airport-ramp.markings-and-signs`

**Question:** You are unsure of your position while taxiing at an unfamiliar airport. What is the right thing to do?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) Keep taxiing until you recognise something
- **B.** (`opt-4`) Turn back the way you came without telling anyone
- **C.** (`opt-3`) Stop where you are, and ask for progressive taxi instructions
- **D.** (`opt-2`) Follow the airplane ahead of you

**Correct answer:** `opt-3`

**Explanation:** Stopping and saying you are unsure is a normal request and controllers handle it every day; taxiing on while hoping to recognise something is how aircraft end up where they should not be. An airport diagram, out and in your hand before you start moving, is what keeps this rare. Confirm in the Aeronautical Information Manual.

**Visual:** An airplane stationary on a taxiway with a question bubble, and an airport diagram drawn on the pilot's lap with the position unmarked. Another aircraft continues ahead into an unmarked area.

## Objective 3 — `s1-airport-ramp.hold-short`

> Explain what hold short means and why it is never optional

### Card 7 — What the instruction means

**Objective ID:** `s1-airport-ramp.hold-short`

**Question:** You are told to hold short of a runway. What exactly are you being told to do?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-4`) Stop before the holding position marking and do not cross it until cleared
- **B.** (`opt-2`) Cross the runway as quickly as you can
- **C.** (`opt-1`) Slow down, and continue if the runway looks clear
- **D.** (`opt-3`) Stop on the runway and wait there

**Correct answer:** `opt-4`

**Explanation:** Hold short means stop on the near side of the marking and stay there until you are told otherwise. Not slow down, not edge forward, not judge it yourself from what you can see. The runway may be being used by an aircraft you cannot see, or by one on a long final you have not spotted. Confirm in the Aeronautical Information Manual.

**Visual:** An airplane stopped short of the marking with its nose clear of the line, and a shaded area beyond it labelled as the protected runway environment.

### Card 8 — Why it is not a judgment call

**Objective ID:** `s1-airport-ramp.hold-short`

**Question:** The runway looks completely empty. Why is crossing without a clearance still wrong?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) Because it wastes the controller's time
- **B.** (`opt-4`) Because the marking is there for airliners, not trainers
- **C.** (`opt-2`) Because it is only wrong at night
- **D.** (`opt-3`) Because you cannot see everything the controller can — traffic on final, an aircraft on a crossing runway, or a vehicle you have not spotted

**Correct answer:** `opt-3`

**Explanation:** Looking is necessary and is not sufficient. The view from a cockpit on the ground is partly blocked by your own airplane, an aircraft on final can be hard to pick out against terrain, and a runway incursion is one of the most serious things that happens on an airport surface. The clearance is what coordinates everyone; your eyes are the backup, not the authority. Confirm in the Aeronautical Information Manual.

**Visual:** A plan view of the airport showing an aircraft on a long final and a vehicle on a crossing taxiway, with a dotted cone marking what the stopped pilot can actually see — neither of them inside it.

### Card 9 — At a field with no tower

**Objective ID:** `s1-airport-ramp.hold-short`

**Question:** There is no tower at this airport, so nobody can clear you onto the runway. What do the holding position markings ask of you?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) Nothing — they apply only where there is a tower
- **B.** (`opt-3`) The same discipline: stop, check the approach and the runway, announce, and only then enter
- **C.** (`opt-4`) That you cross only in daylight
- **D.** (`opt-2`) That you wait for another aircraft to go first

**Correct answer:** `opt-3`

**Explanation:** No tower means nobody is holding the pieces together but you. The marking still marks the boundary of the runway environment, and stopping at it to look down the approach path and announce is exactly the habit that keeps a nontowered field safe. Nothing about an empty frequency proves an empty runway. Confirm in the Aeronautical Information Manual.

**Visual:** A nontowered field with an airplane stopped at the holding position, head turned toward the final approach path, and the sky in that direction drawn with an aircraft small and distant in it.
