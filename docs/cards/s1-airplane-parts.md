# Quiz cards for review — Stage 1, "Airplane parts and what they do"

**Lesson:** `s1-airplane-parts` · **ACS area of operation:** Preflight Preparation · **Sources drawn on:** PHAK

**Status: DRAFT. Not shown to any student until a CFI signs off.**

## What we are asking you to do

Nine cards, three for each of the lesson's three learning objectives. None is marked safety-critical. This is the vocabulary lesson — the one that lets a student describe what they are looking at to you, and later to a mechanic.

Please check three things:

1. **Is it accurate?** Particularly the flap cards, where what is true of a trainer is not true of every airplane.
2. **Are the wrong answers realistic?** Each wrong option is meant to be a real beginner's mental model, not a silly answer.
3. **What should be cut?**

Markers in the text: **`[CFI: confirm value]`** is a number we would not guess. **`FLAG FOR CFI:`** is a specific doubt of ours, written down.

No section numbers, ACS task codes, or figure numbers appear anywhere. Sources are named only.

## About the A–D letters

**The options are shuffled every time a card is shown to a student.** The letters here are display only. Each option carries a stable id, `opt-1` to `opt-4`, and **Correct answer** names that id rather than a letter. Never write an option that refers to another by letter, and there is no "all of the above" or "none of the above".

The ids do not run in alphabetical order down a card. An option keeps its id when the options are reordered.

There is a blank **CFI review** line at the end of every card.

## Objective 1 — `s1-airplane-parts.primary-controls`

> Identify the primary flight controls and the axis each one works about

### Card 1 — The pedals

**Objective ID:** `s1-airplane-parts.primary-controls`

**Question:** The rudder moves the airplane about which axis?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) The longitudinal axis — the airplane rolls
- **B.** (`opt-4`) The lateral axis — the nose moves up or down
- **C.** (`opt-3`) The vertical axis — the nose moves left or right
- **D.** (`opt-2`) No axis — the rudder only steers on the ground

**Correct answer:** `opt-3`

**Explanation:** The rudder moves the nose left and right about the vertical axis, which is yaw. It does steer the nosewheel on many trainers as well, but that is a separate job done by the same pedals. Pairing each control with its axis early makes everything said about turns and stability afterwards easier to follow. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** An airplane drawn from above with a vertical rod through its centre of gravity and the nose swinging either side of the flight path. The rudder is shaded, the other control surfaces drawn plain.

### Card 2 — Why the ailerons need help

**Objective ID:** `s1-airplane-parts.primary-controls`

**Question:** You roll into a turn using the ailerons alone. What tends to happen to the nose?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-4`) It yaws into the turn on its own, so no rudder is needed
- **B.** (`opt-2`) It yaws away from the direction of the turn, because the wing going up meets more drag
- **C.** (`opt-1`) It pitches up sharply
- **D.** (`opt-3`) Nothing happens until the bank passes a large angle

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** Ailerons work in opposite directions, and the one that goes down produces more lift and more drag on that wing — which pulls the nose the other way. That is **adverse yaw**, and it is why the rudder goes in with the aileron rather than being an afterthought. Your instructor will show you what coordinated feels like long before it is something you can describe. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge and the Airplane Flying Handbook.

**Visual:** A plan view of an airplane entering a left turn with the right aileron down, extra drag drawn as a short arrow on the right wing, and the nose shown swinging slightly right of the intended path.

### Card 3 — What the elevator is really changing

**Objective ID:** `s1-airplane-parts.primary-controls`

**Question:** When you pull back on the control, what does the elevator change directly?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-4`) The airplane's altitude, on its own
- **B.** (`opt-1`) The engine's power output
- **C.** (`opt-2`) The airspeed, directly and only
- **D.** (`opt-3`) The airplane's pitch attitude, and with it the wing's angle of attack

**Correct answer:** `opt-3`

**Explanation:** The elevator changes pitch, and pitch changes the angle at which the wing meets the air. Everything else — a change in airspeed, a climb, a descent — follows from that and from what the power is doing. Seeing the elevator as an angle-of-attack control rather than an up-and-down lever is what makes stalls make sense later. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** A side view with the elevator deflected and the chord line rotating relative to the oncoming air, with the angle between them shaded. The altitude of the airplane is deliberately unchanged in the frame.

## Objective 2 — `s1-airplane-parts.flaps`

> Describe what flaps do and when they are used

### Card 4 — What extending flaps gives you

**Objective ID:** `s1-airplane-parts.flaps`

**Question:** What do flaps do for you on an approach?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) They let you fly a steeper approach at a slower speed, with the nose lower and the runway easier to see
- **B.** (`opt-3`) They make the airplane climb better
- **C.** (`opt-2`) They slow the engine down
- **D.** (`opt-4`) They lock the wings in the landing position

**Correct answer:** `opt-1`

**Explanation:** Flaps change the shape of the wing so it produces more lift at a given speed, and they add drag. On an approach that combination is exactly what you want: come down more steeply without speeding up, with a lower nose attitude and a better view of where you are going. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge and the Airplane Flying Handbook.

**Visual:** Two approach paths to the same runway drawn side by side, one shallow with flaps up and one steeper with flaps down, with the nose attitude sketched on each and the pilot's view over the cowling shown beneath.

### Card 5 — When they may not go out

**Objective ID:** `s1-airplane-parts.flaps`

**Question:** What limits when you may extend the flaps?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-2`) Nothing — flaps may be extended at any speed
- **B.** (`opt-1`) A minimum altitude above the ground
- **C.** (`opt-4`) A maximum speed for flap extension, published for your airplane
- **D.** (`opt-3`) A limit on how many times they may be used per flight

**Correct answer:** `opt-4`

**Explanation:** Every airplane has a maximum speed at which the flaps may be extended, and exceeding it risks damaging them or their mechanism — which is why the airspeed indicator has a marked range for flap operation. The figure belongs to your airplane rather than to airplanes in general: `[CFI: confirm value]`. Confirm in the POH or AFM for your airplane and the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** An airspeed indicator with the flap operating range marked as an arc, and beside it a wing with flaps partially extended. No numbers printed on the dial face.

### Card 6 — Flaps on a go-around

**Objective ID:** `s1-airplane-parts.flaps`

**Question:** You go around with full flaps extended. Why are the flaps retracted in stages rather than all at once?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) Because the flap motor cannot move them quickly
- **B.** (`opt-3`) Because retracting them all at once removes lift you are relying on at a low speed
- **C.** (`opt-4`) Because the regulations require it
- **D.** (`opt-2`) Because it makes the airplane quieter for people below

**Correct answer:** `opt-3`

**Explanation:** With full flaps you are flying slowly and leaning on the lift they provide. Taking all of it away at once, close to the ground and before the airplane has accelerated, is how a go-around turns into a sink. The drag they add is real too, which is why they do come up — in stages, on the schedule in your airplane's handbook. Confirm in the Airplane Flying Handbook and the POH or AFM for your airplane.

**Visual:** A climbing path away from a runway with three marks along it, each showing a smaller flap setting than the last, and the airspeed sketched as increasing between them.

## Objective 3 — `s1-airplane-parts.major-components`

> Name the major components of a typical training airplane

### Card 7 — The tail end

**Objective ID:** `s1-airplane-parts.major-components`

**Question:** What is the tail section of the airplane called?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-2`) The fuselage
- **B.** (`opt-1`) The nacelle
- **C.** (`opt-3`) The cowling
- **D.** (`opt-4`) The empennage

**Correct answer:** `opt-4`

**Explanation:** The empennage is the tail assembly. The fuselage is the body, the cowling is the covering around the engine, and a nacelle is a housing that on other aircraft holds an engine. These words earn their keep the first time you have to tell a mechanic which part of the airplane you are talking about. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** A trainer drawn in side view with four leader lines out to labels: fuselage, wing, empennage, cowling. The empennage label is drawn no more prominently than the others.

### Card 8 — What is in the empennage

**Objective ID:** `s1-airplane-parts.major-components`

**Question:** Which surfaces make up the empennage on a typical trainer?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-3`) The horizontal stabilizer with the elevator, and the vertical stabilizer with the rudder
- **B.** (`opt-1`) The ailerons and the flaps
- **C.** (`opt-2`) The landing gear and its fairings
- **D.** (`opt-4`) The propeller and its spinner

**Correct answer:** `opt-3`

**Explanation:** The empennage carries the fixed stabilizers and the moving surfaces attached to them — elevator on the horizontal, rudder on the vertical. Ailerons and flaps live on the wings. Some trainers have a stabilator, a single moving horizontal surface, instead of a separate stabilizer and elevator; your airplane's handbook says which you have. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge and the POH or AFM for your airplane.

**Visual:** A rear three-quarter view of the tail with each surface labelled and the moving parts shaded differently from the fixed ones.

### Card 9 — Why the names matter

**Objective ID:** `s1-airplane-parts.major-components`

**Question:** On your walkaround you find damage. Why does it matter that you can name the part?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-4`) Because the correct name is required before a flight may be cancelled
- **B.** (`opt-2`) So you can describe it precisely to your instructor or a mechanic, and it can be found and assessed
- **C.** (`opt-1`) Because you are expected to repair it yourself
- **D.** (`opt-3`) It does not matter — pointing at it is enough

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** "There is a dent on the leading edge of the left wing, outboard of the strut" gets a useful answer. "Something looks wrong near the front" does not, particularly over the phone. The vocabulary is not for the exam; it is so that what you noticed survives being handed to someone else. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** Two speech bubbles beside the same airplane: one vague and gesturing, one naming the part and its position, with the second bubble drawn reaching a mechanic who is already looking at the right spot.
