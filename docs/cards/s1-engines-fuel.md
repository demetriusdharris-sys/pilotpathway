Nine cards, three for each of the lesson's three learning objectives. Two objectives are marked safety-critical: tracing the fuel system, and verifying fuel quantity and quality.

Please check three things:

1. **Is it accurate for a trainer?** Fuel systems differ between high-wing and low-wing airplanes, and we have tried to write cards that hold for both or that say plainly which one they mean.
2. **Are the wrong answers realistic?** Each wrong option is meant to be a real student misunderstanding.
3. **What should be cut?**

## About the A–D letters

**The options are shuffled every time a card is shown to a student.** The letters here are display only. The ids do not run in alphabetical order down a card. An option keeps its id when the options are reordered.

## Objective 1 — `s1-engines-fuel.fuel-system-path`

> Trace the fuel system from tank to engine on a typical trainer

### Card 1 — What the selector does

**Objective ID:** `s1-engines-fuel.fuel-system-path`

**Question:** What is the fuel selector's job?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-4`) It sets how much fuel the engine burns per hour
- **B.** (`opt-2`) It chooses which tank or tanks feed the engine, and it can shut the fuel off
- **C.** (`opt-1`) It measures how much fuel is left
- **D.** (`opt-3`) It primes the engine for starting

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** The selector routes fuel — one tank, the other, sometimes both — and gives you a way to cut it off entirely, which matters in a fire or a fuel leak. Mixture, priming and quantity are all somebody else's job. Which positions your airplane has, and which it should be in for each phase of flight, comes from its own checklist. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge and the POH or AFM for your airplane.

**Visual:** A simplified fuel path drawn left to right — two tanks, the selector, a strainer, and the engine — with the selector shaded and its positions drawn as a small dial beside it.

### Card 2 — Why there is more than one drain

**Objective ID:** `s1-engines-fuel.fuel-system-path`

**Question:** Why does a trainer have several fuel drains rather than one?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) So fuel can be emptied quickly for maintenance
- **B.** (`opt-3`) To speed up refuelling
- **C.** (`opt-2`) Because the regulations set a number of drains per tank
- **D.** (`opt-4`) Because water and sediment collect at the low point of each part of the system, so each drain is a separate check

**Correct answer:** `opt-4`

**Explanation:** Water is heavier than fuel and settles at the lowest point it can reach — which is different in each tank and again at the strainer. Sampling one and skipping the others tests only part of the system. How many drains your airplane has and where they are is in its handbook, and your instructor will walk you round them. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge and the POH or AFM for your airplane.

**Visual:** A cutaway of a wing tank and a strainer bowl, each with water drawn as a darker layer settled at its own lowest point, and a sample cup beneath each drain.

### Card 3 — The fuel you cannot use

**Objective ID:** `s1-engines-fuel.fuel-system-path`

**Question:** What does "unusable fuel" mean?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) Fuel that is in the tank but that the engine cannot reliably draw on in flight
- **B.** (`opt-3`) Fuel that has gone bad through age
- **C.** (`opt-2`) Fuel of the wrong grade for the engine
- **D.** (`opt-4`) Fuel held back deliberately as a legal reserve

**Correct answer:** `opt-1`

**Explanation:** Part of what is in the tanks cannot be counted on — it sits below the pickup, or cannot be drawn in every attitude. What the handbook calls **usable fuel** is what you plan with, and it is less than the tanks hold. This is also why a gauge reading just above empty is not a quantity you can fly on. Your airplane's usable fuel is in its handbook: `[CFI: confirm value]`. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge and the POH or AFM for your airplane.

**Visual:** A tank in cross-section with the fuel level drawn, the pickup point marked partway up, and the fuel below it shaded differently and labelled as not available in flight.

## Objective 2 — `s1-engines-fuel.magnetos-and-runup`

> Explain why there are two magnetos and what the runup check confirms

### Card 4 — Why two

**Objective ID:** `s1-engines-fuel.magnetos-and-runup`

**Question:** Why does a piston trainer have two magnetos?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-4`) So one can be saved as a spare and only used in an emergency
- **B.** (`opt-2`) Because the engine cannot start on one alone
- **C.** (`opt-1`) So the engine keeps running if one fails, and because two sparks per cylinder burn the mixture more effectively
- **D.** (`opt-3`) Because one drives the starter and the other the alternator

**Correct answer:** `opt-1`

**Explanation:** Two ignition systems, two spark plugs in each cylinder — one so a failure does not stop the engine, and one because lighting the mixture from two places burns it more completely and gives more power. Both run all the time; neither is a spare sitting idle. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** A cylinder drawn with two plugs, each traced back to its own magneto, with two flame fronts meeting in the middle. Beside it, the same cylinder with one path greyed out and the engine still drawn running.

### Card 5 — What the mag check is asking

**Objective ID:** `s1-engines-fuel.magnetos-and-runup`

**Question:** During the runup you switch to one magneto and the engine speed drops slightly, then recovers when you switch back. What does that tell you?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-4`) That the magneto you selected has failed
- **B.** (`opt-2`) That the engine is running on both systems and each one works — a small drop is what you expect
- **C.** (`opt-3`) That the engine is running too rich
- **D.** (`opt-1`) That the propeller needs adjusting

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** On one magneto the mixture burns less efficiently, so a small drop is normal and is evidence that the system you selected is doing something. What matters is whether the drop is within the limits for your airplane, and whether the two sides are close to each other — **and no drop at all is its own problem**, because it can mean the magneto was never switched off. The acceptable drop and the allowable difference are aircraft-specific: `[CFI: confirm value]`. Confirm in the POH or AFM for your airplane and the Airplane Flying Handbook.

**Visual:** A tachometer drawn three times — both magnetos, left only, right only — with the needle a little lower in the two single-magneto frames and the difference between them marked as the thing to compare.

### Card 6 — The propeller does not know the switches are off

**Objective ID:** `s1-engines-fuel.magnetos-and-runup`

**Question:** The master switch is off and the key is out. Why is the propeller still treated as dangerous?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-3`) Because the engine may still be hot
- **B.** (`opt-4`) Because the airplane may roll if it is not chocked
- **C.** (`opt-2`) Because static electricity builds up on the blades
- **D.** (`opt-1`) Because a magneto makes its own spark, so a fault in the wiring that should ground it can leave the ignition live

**Correct answer:** `opt-1`

**Explanation:** A magneto generates its own spark and does not need the battery — which is why the engine keeps running when electrical power is lost, and why the switches work by *grounding* the magnetos to stop them. If that grounding path is broken, the ignition is live regardless of the switches. Treat every propeller as capable of turning, always, without exception. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** A magneto drawn with its own spark path and no connection to the battery, and the grounding wire shown as the thing that switches it off — drawn with a break in it, and the plug sparking anyway.

## Objective 3 — `s1-engines-fuel.verify-fuel`

> Describe how to verify fuel quantity and quality before flight

### Card 7 — What you are looking for in the sample

**Objective ID:** `s1-engines-fuel.verify-fuel`

**Question:** You have drawn a fuel sample into a clear cup. What are you checking?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-2`) That there is no water or sediment in it, and that it is the colour and smell of the right grade for your airplane
- **B.** (`opt-4`) Only that there is fuel in the system at all
- **C.** (`opt-1`) That it evaporates quickly when poured out
- **D.** (`opt-3`) That its temperature matches the outside air

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** Three things at once: water, which sits as a separate layer at the bottom and can stop an engine; solid contamination; and whether this is the fuel your airplane is supposed to be burning, which you judge by colour and smell. Fuel grades are dyed different colours precisely so the wrong one is visible. The grade your airplane takes, and the colour it should be, are `[CFI: confirm value]`. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge and the POH or AFM for your airplane.

**Visual:** A clear sample cup held against the light with a distinct lower layer of water and a speck of sediment, beside a second cup of clean fuel — both drawn the same size, with the difference in the contents rather than in the drawing.

### Card 8 — The wrong fuel in the truck

**Objective ID:** `s1-engines-fuel.verify-fuel`

**Question:** Someone offers to top your trainer up with a fuel of a different grade from the one it takes. What is the right answer?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) Accept it if it is a higher grade, since that is an improvement
- **B.** (`opt-3`) Accept it and note it in the maintenance log
- **C.** (`opt-4`) No — the airplane takes the grade in its handbook, and anything else is a maintenance matter, not a pilot decision
- **D.** (`opt-2`) Accept it if the flight is a short one

**Correct answer:** `opt-4`

**Explanation:** The fuel an engine is approved for is part of its approved design, and fuelling an airplane with something else — including a fuel meant for turbine engines, which has happened and has killed people — is not a judgment call at the pump. Watch the fuelling if you can, check the truck, and check your sample afterwards. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge and the POH or AFM for your airplane.

**Visual:** A fuel truck placard and an airplane's fuel filler placard drawn side by side at the same size, with the two labels differing — and the pilot's hand raised between them.

### Card 9 — Enough to get there is not enough

**Objective ID:** `s1-engines-fuel.verify-fuel`

**Question:** You have worked out that your usable fuel covers the flight exactly. What is missing from that plan?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) A reserve, and an allowance for the wind, a diversion, or a hold not going your way
- **B.** (`opt-3`) Nothing — arriving with the tanks nearly empty is efficient
- **C.** (`opt-2`) A calculation of the aircraft's empty weight
- **D.** (`opt-4`) An estimate of how much fuel evaporates in flight

**Correct answer:** `opt-1`

**Explanation:** Fuel planning works from usable fuel and a burn rate, and then adds what the regulations require you to land with, plus whatever the day suggests — headwinds that were stronger than forecast, a runway that closes, a hold. Fuel exhaustion accidents are usually not mysteries; they are plans with no margin that then met an ordinary surprise. The required reserve is `[CFI: confirm value]`. Confirm in 14 CFR and the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** A horizontal fuel bar divided into three labelled segments — the flight, the reserve, and an unlabelled margin — with an arrow showing the planned flight expanding into the second segment when the wind is stronger than forecast.
