## What we are asking you to do

Nine cards, three for each of the lesson's three learning objectives. The middle objective — basic VFR weather minimums — is marked safety-critical.

**One thing to decide before anything else on this lesson.** Our standing rule is that we do not print a number unless we are certain of it and it is the same everywhere, because a half-remembered figure carried into a cockpit or an oral exam is worse than no figure. That rule collides with this lesson: airspace dimensions and VFR minimums *are* numbers, and the objective says a student should be able to state them.

So these cards test the shape of the thing — what each class is for, who you talk to, what changes with altitude, and where to look it up — and every specific figure is left as a gap. **Please tell us which way to go from here:** give us the set of figures you teach and we will write cards that use them, or tell us this objective should be assessed from the chart and the regulation rather than from memory. Either answer is workable; guessing is not.

Please also check:

1. **Is it accurate?** Especially the entry requirements, where "clearance" and "communication established" are different things and students blur them.
2. **Are the wrong answers realistic?** Each is meant to be a belief a student pilot actually holds.
3. **What should be cut?**

Markers in the text: **`[CFI: confirm value]`** is a number we would not guess. **`FLAG FOR CFI:`** is a specific doubt of ours, written down.

No section numbers, ACS task codes, or figure numbers appear anywhere. Sources are named only.

## About the A–D letters

**The options are shuffled every time a card is shown to a student.** The letters here are display only. Each option carries a stable id, `opt-1` to `opt-4`, and **Correct answer** names that id rather than a letter. Never write an option that refers to another by letter, and there is no "all of the above" or "none of the above".

The ids do not run in alphabetical order down a card. An option keeps its id when the options are reordered.

There is a blank **CFI review** line at the end of every card.

## Objective 1 — `s1-airspace-intro.airspace-classes`

> Describe the dimensions and entry requirements of each airspace class

### Card 1 — Getting into Class B

**Objective ID:** `s1-airspace-intro.airspace-classes`

**Question:** What do you need before you may enter Class B airspace?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) Two-way radio contact, which is enough on its own
- **B.** (`opt-2`) A clearance from ATC — specifically, to be told you are cleared into the Class B
- **C.** (`opt-4`) A working transponder, which is enough on its own
- **D.** (`opt-3`) An announcement of your position before you cross the boundary

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** Class B is the one place where talking is not enough: you need an actual clearance into the airspace, and hearing your call sign answered is not the same as being cleared in. Equipment requirements apply too, but they do not substitute for the clearance. If you have not heard the words, you are not cleared. Confirm in 14 CFR and the Aeronautical Information Manual.

**Visual:** The layered shape of Class B drawn in profile, with an airplane holding outside the boundary and a speech bubble from the ground containing the word "cleared". The airplane's path only crosses the boundary after the bubble.

### Card 2 — Getting into Class D

**Objective ID:** `s1-airspace-intro.airspace-classes`

**Question:** The tower is open at a Class D airport you are flying to. What has to happen before you enter its airspace?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-3`) You must be issued a clearance into the airspace, as in Class B
- **B.** (`opt-4`) You must squawk a code the tower assigns you
- **C.** (`opt-2`) Nothing — you announce your position and join the pattern
- **D.** (`opt-1`) Two-way radio communication must be established with the tower

**Correct answer:** `opt-1`

**Explanation:** Class D asks for two-way radio communication to be established, which happens when the controller answers you using your call sign. A reply of "aircraft calling, stand by" is not it. That difference between **established communication** and **a clearance** is worth getting straight early, because it is the difference between Class D and Class B. Confirm in 14 CFR and the Aeronautical Information Manual.

**Visual:** Two speech bubbles side by side: one reading a call sign spoken back by the tower, marked as communication established; one reading "aircraft calling, stand by", marked as not. The airspace boundary is drawn beneath both.

### Card 3 — Why the classes differ at all

**Objective ID:** `s1-airspace-intro.airspace-classes`

**Question:** What decides which class of airspace surrounds a particular airport?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-4`) The length of its longest runway
- **B.** (`opt-1`) Its elevation above sea level
- **C.** (`opt-2`) The volume and type of traffic it handles, and the air traffic services provided there
- **D.** (`opt-3`) Whether it sits inside a city boundary

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** Airspace classes describe how much air traffic service is provided and what is being protected — busy airline airports at the top, quiet fields with no tower at the bottom. Once you see the classes as levels of service rather than as arbitrary letters, what each one asks of you starts to make sense. The dimensions of each class are worth learning from the chart and the regulation: `[CFI: confirm value]`. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge and the Aeronautical Information Manual.

**Visual:** A stepped diagram from a quiet grass strip up to a major airport, with the level of service drawn as increasing detail — no controller, a tower, radar — rather than as letters alone. Letters appear alongside, not as the organising idea.

## Objective 2 — `s1-airspace-intro.vfr-minimums`

> State the basic VFR weather minimums a student pilot will use

### Card 4 — Why the minimums are not one number

**Objective ID:** `s1-airspace-intro.vfr-minimums`

**Question:** Why are the VFR visibility and cloud clearance requirements different in different places?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) They change with the airspace class and your altitude, because those change how much warning you get of other traffic
- **B.** (`opt-3`) They change with the season
- **C.** (`opt-2`) They change with the aircraft you are flying
- **D.** (`opt-4`) They are the same everywhere, and the variation is a myth

**Correct answer:** `opt-1`

**Explanation:** The requirements exist so you can see and be seen in time to do something about it. Where traffic is separated for you and where everyone is fast and close to cloud, the numbers change accordingly — which is why they are tied to airspace class and altitude rather than being one figure to memorise. The actual figures are worth learning as a set: `[CFI: confirm value]`. Confirm in 14 CFR and the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.

**Visual:** A single airplane drawn three times at different altitudes beside a cloud, with the distance it must keep drawn as a shaded standoff that changes size. No figures on the shading.

### Card 5 — Legal and allowed are not the same

**Objective ID:** `s1-airspace-intro.vfr-minimums`

**Question:** The regulation would permit the flight in today's visibility. Your instructor's endorsement sets a higher figure. Which one governs?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-2`) The regulation, since it is the law
- **B.** (`opt-4`) The endorsement — your own limitations are stricter, and they are what you fly to
- **C.** (`opt-1`) Whichever you can better justify afterwards
- **D.** (`opt-3`) Neither, if the tower says the weather is fine

**Correct answer:** `opt-4`

**Explanation:** The regulation is a floor, not a target, and the limits written on your endorsement sit above it. Later, as a certificated pilot, you will set personal minimums of your own for the same reason: the regulation describes the worst conditions in which flight is permitted, not the conditions in which you are at your best. Confirm in 14 CFR and with your CFI.

**Visual:** Three horizontal lines stacked: the regulation at the bottom, the endorsement above it, today's weather drawn as a band that sits between them. The aircraft symbol rests on the endorsement line, not the lowest one.

### Card 6 — The visibility is going down

**Objective ID:** `s1-airspace-intro.vfr-minimums`

**Question:** En route, the visibility ahead is worsening and is heading toward your limit. What is the right move?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-2`) Continue and see whether it improves past the next ridge
- **B.** (`opt-1`) Descend lower so you can keep the ground in sight
- **C.** (`opt-4`) Climb through the layer to the clear air above
- **D.** (`opt-3`) Turn back or land while you still have the conditions to do it in

**Correct answer:** `opt-3`

**Explanation:** Deteriorating visibility takes away your options faster than it takes away your altitude, and every one of the other answers is a step further into a situation that is already closing. Deciding early — turning back, landing, or not going at all — is what keeps this a story about a changed plan. Continuing VFR into deteriorating weather is one of the most studied ways pilots come to harm. Confirm in the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge and the Aeronautical Information Manual.

**Visual:** A route drawn left to right with the air ahead progressively greyed out. Two paths branch early: one turning back into clear air, one continuing into the grey and narrowing to nothing. The branch point is drawn well before the grey begins.

## Objective 3 — `s1-airspace-intro.sectional-boundaries`

> Identify airspace boundaries on a sectional chart

### Card 7 — The dashed blue line

**Objective ID:** `s1-airspace-intro.sectional-boundaries`

**Question:** On a sectional chart, a dashed blue line encircles an airport. What does it mark?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-3`) Class D airspace
- **B.** (`opt-1`) A military operations area
- **C.** (`opt-4`) A prohibited area
- **D.** (`opt-2`) The airport's traffic pattern

**Correct answer:** `opt-3`

**Explanation:** Sectional charts mark each class of airspace with its own line style and colour, and the dashed blue ring is Class D. Learning the symbols as a set — and checking the chart's own legend rather than trusting memory — is how you tell at a glance whose airspace you are about to enter. Confirm in the Aeronautical Information Manual and the chart legend.

**Visual:** A small patch of sectional chart drawn plainly, with one airport ringed by a dashed blue line and the chart legend beside it, the matching legend entry circled.

### Card 8 — The numbers inside the ring

**Objective ID:** `s1-airspace-intro.sectional-boundaries`

**Question:** Inside a segment of controlled airspace, the chart prints one number above another. What are they telling you?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-4`) The airport elevation and the pattern altitude
- **B.** (`opt-1`) The tower frequency, split across two lines
- **C.** (`opt-2`) The top and the bottom of that piece of airspace
- **D.** (`opt-3`) The highest terrain and the highest obstacle nearby

**Correct answer:** `opt-2`

**Explanation:** The stacked figures give the ceiling and the floor of that segment, so you can tell whether your planned altitude puts you inside it or underneath it. Airspace is three-dimensional, and flying beneath a shelf rather than through it is a normal, legitimate way to route around it. The units and conventions are on the chart legend: `[CFI: confirm value]`. Confirm in the Aeronautical Information Manual and the chart legend.

**Visual:** A profile view beside a plan view of the same airspace shelf, with an airplane routed underneath the shelf in the profile and its position shown inside the ring in the plan view — the same flight, legal in one reading and confusing in the other.

### Card 9 — What the class letters do not tell you

**Objective ID:** `s1-airspace-intro.sectional-boundaries`

**Question:** You have checked the airspace classes along your route. What else on the chart still needs looking at?

**Options:**

- **A.** (`opt-1`) Nothing — the classes cover everything that restricts you
- **B.** (`opt-3`) Special use airspace, such as restricted areas and military operations areas — and any temporary restrictions, which are not printed on the chart at all
- **C.** (`opt-4`) Only the airports you plan to land at
- **D.** (`opt-2`) Only the terrain elevations

**Correct answer:** `opt-3`

**Explanation:** Special use airspace sits on the chart in its own symbols and is not described by the class letters, and temporary flight restrictions do not appear on a printed chart at all — they come from your preflight briefing. Both can turn a perfectly planned route into a call from the FAA. Confirm in the Aeronautical Information Manual and your preflight weather briefing.

**Visual:** A chart segment showing a hatched special use area crossing a planned route line, and beside it a separate briefing sheet with a temporary restriction drawn on it — deliberately not on the chart, to make the point that the chart alone is not the whole picture.
